    Chapter 5 — Advanced Broadband Wireless Access & Services¶

    Chapter at a glance

    Contact hours: 3 lecture · Home study: 7 h · Part IV — Emerging technologies

    Coverage (syllabus): 4G technologies · 4G features and challenges · technology path · LTE and WiMAX · network architecture · capacity of WiMAX · radio and network components.

    Learning outcomes¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

    1. State the IMT-Advanced requirements for 4G and how LTE/LTE-Advanced and WiMAX meet them.
    2. Explain OFDMA (downlink) and SC-FDMA (uplink) — the PHY foundation of LTE — and contrast with WCDMA.
    3. Compute LTE resource block capacity, peak throughput, and spectral efficiency from modulation, coding, MIMO, and bandwidth parameters.
    4. Describe MIMO — spatial multiplexing, transmit diversity, beamforming — and compute MIMO capacity gain.
    5. Draw and describe the 4G all-IP architecture (EPC) — eNodeB, MME, S-GW, P-GW, HSS — and identify the role of each.
    6. Explain LTE-Advanced enhancements — carrier aggregation, higher-order MIMO, CoMP, relays — and their contribution to peak rates.
    7. Describe WiMAX architecture and PHY, compute its capacity, and compare with LTE.
    8. Explain radio and network components — remote radio heads, baseband units, C-RAN, distributed antenna systems — and their role in modern 4G/5G deployments.
    9. Analyse network capacity and design — spectral efficiency, cell-edge performance, and the "capacity per MHz per km²" metric.

    5.1 The 4G vision and requirements¶

    5.1.1 What is 4G?¶

    4G is the generation of mobile systems defined by the ITU-R IMT-Advanced requirements (2008), intended to succeed IMT-2000 (3G) with a fundamental leap in capability:

    Requirement Value Notes
    Peak downlink 1 Gbps (low mobility), 100 Mbps (high mobility) Nominal for stationary and driving
    Peak uplink 500 Mbps (low mobility)
    Spectral efficiency 15 bps/Hz DL, 6.75 bps/Hz UL Peak, per cell
    Bandwidth Up to 100 MHz (aggregated) Flexible
    Latency (user plane) < 10 ms For interactive services
    Latency (control plane) < 100 ms Idle → connected
    Mobility Up to 350 km/h With acceptable quality
    All-IP Yes No circuit switching
    Coexistence With 2G/3G Via multi-mode UEs

    5.1.2 Technology path¶

    Three standards competed and evolved to meet the 4G requirements:

    Standard 3G predecessor Developed by First commercial
    LTE (Long Term Evolution) UMTS (WCDMA) 3GPP 2009
    LTE-Advanced LTE 3GPP 2013
    WiMAX (Mobile WiMAX) IEEE 802.16e IEEE 2006
    WiMAX 2 (WirelessMAN-Advanced) IEEE 802.16m IEEE 2011

    Key evolution milestones:

    • 2004 — 3GPP starts LTE study (Release 8).
    • 2006 — First Mobile WiMAX deployment (South Korea, "WiBro").
    • 2008 — LTE Release 8 frozen; IMT-Advanced requirements published.
    • 2009 — First commercial LTE network (TeliaSonera, Stockholm and Oslo).
    • 2011 — LTE-Advanced (Release 10) frozen.
    • 2013 — LTE-Advanced commercial (SK Telecom, 150 Mbps).
    • 2018 — LTE dominates global mobile subscriptions.

    The market outcome: LTE won globally. WiMAX, despite an early technical lead, lost to LTE because of ecosystem effects — LTE benefited from the massive global GSM/UMTS operator base, standardized evolution path, and dual-mode devices that fell back to GSM/UMTS gracefully. WiMAX operators had to build networks from scratch and had fewer devices.

    By 2018, WiMAX was commercially dead; LTE carried ~70% of global mobile traffic.

    5.1.3 4G challenges¶

    The transition from 3G to 4G posed engineering challenges that shaped the design:

    1. Spectrum scarcity. 3G's 5 MHz carrier couldn't deliver 100 Mbps. 4G needed 20 MHz, later 100+ MHz via carrier aggregation.

    2. Flat architecture. 3G's hierarchical RNC/BSC added latency. 4G removed the RNC — the eNodeB talks directly to the core network. This flattens the architecture and reduces round-trip latency from ~50 ms to ~10 ms.

    3. All-IP. Circuit switching is gone — even voice is carried over IP (VoLTE, Voice over LTE) with SIP signalling and dedicated QoS bearers.

    4. MIMO. The only way to reach Gbps-class rates at reasonable SNR is MIMO (multiple-input multiple-output) — 4×4 or 8×8 antenna arrays exploiting spatial multiplexing.

    5. Flexible spectrum. 4G must work in paired (FDD) and unpaired (TDD) spectrum, with variable bandwidths (1.4, 3, 5, 10, 15, 20 MHz per carrier).

    5.2 OFDMA and SC-FDMA — the new PHY¶

    5.2.1 Why not WCDMA?¶

    WCDMA (Chapter 4) works well for moderate data rates, but at 100 Mbps it hits fundamental limits:

    Limitation Consequence at high rate
    Spread signal The 5 MHz carrier can only support ~5 bps/Hz (Shannon); 100 Mbps needs >20 MHz
    Complex Rake receiver Becomes impractical at very high chip rates
    Self-interference At very high SNR, interference (not noise) dominates — difficult to overcome
    Slow channel estimation Rake combining requires fast adaptation; scaling is hard

    OFDMA replaces CDMA's time-domain spreading with frequency-domain parallelism — essentially the "right tool" for high-rate broadband channels.

    5.2.2 OFDMA (downlink)¶

    OFDMA = Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiple Access — a multi-user version of OFDM (Chapter 2).

    Key idea: the total bandwidth is divided into narrow subcarriers, and each subcarrier is allocated dynamically to whichever UE has the best channel at that frequency.

    LTE resource grid:

    • Subcarrier spacing: \(\Delta f = 15\) kHz (also 7.5 kHz for MBSFN; 3.75 kHz for NB-IoT).
    • Resource element (RE): one subcarrier × one OFDM symbol.
    • Resource block (RB): 12 subcarriers × 7 symbols (normal CP) = 84 REs, or 12 × 6 = 72 REs (extended CP). 1 RB = 180 kHz × 0.5 ms slot.

    Time-frequency structure:

    Level Duration Contents
    OFDM symbol 71.4 μs (normal CP) One QAM symbol per subcarrier
    Slot 0.5 ms (7 symbols) 7 OFDM symbols
    Subframe 1 ms (2 slots) 14 symbols
    Radio frame 10 ms (10 subframes) 20 slots
    Superframe — (In LTE-A carrier aggregation, multiple frames)

    The scheduler (in the eNodeB) allocates RBs every 1 ms (per subframe) to each UE. This is dynamic, frequency-selective scheduling — the scheduler picks the best RBs for each UE each TTI (transmission-time interval).

    Frequency diversity: by allocating non-adjacent RBs to a UE (frequency-selective scheduling), the scheduler mitigates fading on specific subcarriers.

    5.2.3 SC-FDMA (uplink)¶

    SC-FDMA = Single-Carrier FDMA — used in the uplink because of a PAPR problem with OFDMA.

    Why not OFDMA in the uplink?

    • UE battery power is limited — the PA must operate efficiently.
    • OFDMA's PAPR (10–12 dB) would require the UE PA to back off 10+ dB → very inefficient → short battery life and limited uplink range.

    SC-FDMA applies a DFT precoding step before IFFT:

    \[ \boxed{\;\text{data} \to \text{DFT (size M)} \to \text{subcarrier mapping} \to \text{IFFT (size N)} \to \text{CP} \to \text{transmit}\;} \]

    The effect: the transmitted signal is effectively a single-carrier signal with PAPR ~3–6 dB — much closer to a constant envelope, allowing the UE PA to operate near saturation.

    Trade-offs:

    Property OFDMA SC-FDMA
    PAPR 10–12 dB 3–6 dB
    UE PA efficiency Low High
    Frequency scheduling flexibility High (any RB) Moderate (must be contiguous RBs)
    Receiver complexity (equalization) Low Higher (DFT-spread)
    Spectral efficiency High Slightly lower
    Used in LTE Downlink Uplink
    Used in 5G NR Downlink AND uplink (SA) Optional uplink (coverage)

    Worked example 5.1 — LTE resource block capacity¶

    Scenario: LTE 20 MHz carrier, normal CP, 2×2 MIMO downlink, 64-QAM, rate-3/4 coding.

    Step 1 — RBs per subframe:

    • 20 MHz channel = 100 RBs (each RB is 180 kHz; 100 × 180 = 18 MHz used of 20 MHz; 2 MHz is guard band).
    • Per subframe (1 ms): 100 RBs.

    Step 2 — REs per subframe:

    • 12 subcarriers × 7 symbols × 2 slots = 168 REs per RB per subframe.
    • But LTE reserves some REs for reference signals (pilots), PDCCH (control), and PBCH / PSS / SSS (sync). Typically ~30% overhead for control.
    • Usable REs for data: ~120 REs per RB per subframe (approximately 85% efficiency).

    Step 3 — Bits per subframe:

    • Per RE: 64-QAM = 6 bits/symbol; rate-3/4 coding → 4.5 bits per RE.
    • Per RB: 120 REs × 4.5 = 540 bits per RB.
    • Per subframe: 100 RBs × 540 = 54,000 bits per subframe.

    Step 4 — Peak data rate:

    • 1 ms subframes → 1000 subframes per second.
    • Single-stream: 54,000 × 1000 = 54 Mbps.
    • With 2×2 MIMO (2 spatial streams): 108 Mbps.

    Comparison with 3GPP LTE Cat-4 (150 Mbps): The 108 Mbps estimate is close; the standard quote of 150 Mbps assumes: 2×2 MIMO, 20 MHz, 64-QAM, rate-1 (before coding), and slightly higher reference-signal efficiency. With rate-3/4 coding, practical peak is ~110–130 Mbps.

    Worked example 5.2 — SC-FDMA vs OFDMA PAPR trade-off¶

    Scenario: 10 MHz uplink channel. A UE with a 23 dBm PA and 30 dBm max power.

    With OFDMA:

    • PAPR of 10 dB → peak power 33 dBm.
    • PA must back off to 20 dBm average → effective radiated power 20 dBm.
    • Coverage limited: uplink cell radius at 20 dBm.

    With SC-FDMA:

    • PAPR of 4 dB → peak power 27 dBm.
    • PA can operate at 26 dBm average → +6 dB uplink power.
    • Coverage: ~2× the cell radius (path-loss exponent 3.5: 6 dB ≈ 2× radius).

    Why LTE chose SC-FDMA for uplink: 6 dB uplink gain is worth ~2× coverage — the difference between a UE that works indoors and one that doesn't. Downlink uses OFDMA because the eNodeB (not the UE) transmits, with no battery constraint.

    5.3 MIMO — the spatial dimension¶

    MIMO (Multiple-Input Multiple-Output) is the technology that makes gigabit wireless feasible. By using multiple antennas at both transmitter and receiver, MIMO enables:

    • Spatial multiplexing — transmit independent data streams simultaneously.
    • Transmit diversity — combine signals from multiple antennas for reliability.
    • Beamforming — focus energy toward a specific UE.
    • Multi-user MIMO — serve multiple UEs simultaneously on the same resource.

    5.3.1 Spatial multiplexing¶

    With \(N_t\) transmit antennas and \(N_r\) receive antennas, the channel is an \(N_r \times N_t\) matrix \(H\). If the paths are sufficiently uncorrelated, the capacity grows linearly with \(\min(N_t, N_r)\):

    \[ \boxed{\;C = B \log_2\det\!\left(I + \frac{\text{SNR}}{N_t}\, H H^\dagger\right)\;} \]

    For an i.i.d. Rayleigh channel, this simplifies at high SNR to:

    \[ \boxed{\;C \approx \min(N_t, N_r) \cdot B \log_2(\text{SNR}) + \text{constant}\;} \]

    2×2 MIMO at high SNR ≈ 2× the capacity of SISO. This is the spatial multiplexing gain — the single biggest reason LTE achieves 100+ Mbps per 20 MHz.

    Requirements:

    • Rich scattering — paths must be uncorrelated (typical urban environment).
    • Antenna spacing ≥ λ/2 (or cross-polarized antennas).
    • Rank of the channel matrix = min(N_t, N_r) (for full multiplexing).

    LTE MIMO configurations:

    Configuration Peak streams Typical use
    1×1 (SISO) 1 Fallback
    2×2 (MIMO) 2 Standard LTE Cat-3/4
    4×4 (MIMO) 4 LTE-Advanced Cat-6+
    8×8 (MIMO) 8 LTE-Advanced Pro

    5.3.2 Transmit diversity¶

    When the channel is rank-deficient (e.g., single dominant path, correlated antennas), spatial multiplexing fails. Transmit diversity instead uses the multiple antennas for reliability — sending the same information via orthogonal codes so the receiver combines them.

    Alamouti scheme (2×1 MIMO):

    \[ \boxed{\;s_1 \to (x_1, x_2),\qquad s_2 \to (-x_2^*, x_1^*)\;} \]

    The two symbols are transmitted from the two antennas in two consecutive time slots. The receiver combines them to achieve 2nd-order diversity — a 3 dB power gain in Rayleigh fading at the cost of half the spatial multiplexing rate.

    SFBC (Space-Frequency Block Coding) — the LTE version of Alamouti, applied over adjacent subcarriers instead of adjacent time slots.

    Use: PDSCH (data channel) when spatial multiplexing isn't possible; PBCH (broadcast), PDCCH (control), PSS/SSS (sync).

    5.3.3 Beamforming¶

    Beamforming uses multiple antennas at the transmitter to focus energy toward a specific UE:

    \[ \boxed{\;\mathbf{w} = \frac{\mathbf{h}^*}{\|\mathbf{h}\|},\quad y = \mathbf{h}^\top \mathbf{w}\, s = \|\mathbf{h}\|\, s\;.} \]

    Gain: \(10\log_{10}(N_t)\) dB in SNR — for 8 antennas, 9 dB.

    Used for:

    • Coverage extension at cell edge.
    • Interference reduction — the beam doesn't radiate toward other cells.
    • Multi-user MIMO — separate beams to different UEs.

    LTE MIMO mode 7 (TM7) supports single-layer beamforming; TM8 supports dual-layer beamforming (for MU-MIMO); 5G massive MIMO uses beamforming with 64–256 antennas.

    Worked example 5.3 — MIMO capacity gain¶

    Scenario: SNR = 20 dB (linear 100), 20 MHz channel.

    SISO: \(C = 20 \times 10^6 \times \log_2(101) \approx 133\) Mbps.

    2×2 MIMO (i.i.d. Rayleigh, high SNR): \(C \approx 2 \times 133 = 266\) Mbps.

    4×4 MIMO (i.i.d. Rayleigh, high SNR): \(C \approx 4 \times 133 = 532\) Mbps.

    8×8 MIMO: \(C \approx 8 \times 133 = 1064\) Mbps.

    In practice:

    • 2×2 MIMO: ~1.7× gain (not quite 2× due to correlation and pilot overhead).
    • 4×4 MIMO: ~3× gain in good conditions.
    • 8×8 MIMO: used only in LTE-A Pro and 5G NR, in dense urban / indoor.

    The gain is not quite linear because:

    • Reference signals (pilots) for channel estimation consume overhead, scaling with \(N_t\).
    • Antenna correlation reduces the channel rank.
    • Higher-order modulation is harder to decode with more streams.

    Still, MIMO is the workhorse of 4G/5G spectral efficiency.

    5.3.4 Multi-user MIMO¶

    Rather than serving one UE with multiple streams, MU-MIMO serves multiple UEs simultaneously on the same time-frequency resource, using spatial separation:

    • eNodeB has \(N_t\) antennas.
    • Each UE has 1–2 antennas.
    • The eNodeB separates UEs by precoding — weighting each UE's signal so they don't interfere (zero-forcing or MMSE precoding).

    Gain: For \(K\) UEs served simultaneously, uplink capacity scales with \(\min(N_t, K)\) — the same multiplexing gain, but shared across users.

    LTE TM8 supports downlink MU-MIMO (up to 4 UEs); 5G massive MIMO supports 16+ UEs simultaneously.

    5.4 LTE architecture — the Evolved Packet Core (EPC)¶

    The LTE architecture reflects a fundamental redesign of the core network — the EPC (Evolved Packet Core) — with flat, all-IP architecture.

    5.4.1 Flat architecture¶

    Compared with 3G (UMTS), LTE removes the RNC entirely:

    Aspect UMTS LTE
    Base station NodeB + RNC (two separate nodes) eNodeB (single node handles PHY, MAC, RRC, and user-plane forwarding)
    Radio resource control RNC eNodeB (was RNC)
    Handover control RNC eNodeB ↔ eNodeB via X2
    Latency ~50 ms ~10 ms
    Interfaces Iu, Iub, Iur S1 (to core), X2 (between eNodeBs)

    The eNodeB now performs RNC functions plus base station functions — a single node covering PHY, MAC, RLC, PDCP, and RRC. This flattening dramatically reduces latency and simplifies operations.

    5.4.2 Network elements¶

    eNodeB (Evolved Node B) — the 4G base station.

    • All radio access functions: PHY, MAC, RLC, PDCP, RRC.
    • User-plane: forwards IP packets between UE and S-GW.
    • Control-plane: manages RRC signalling, radio bearer setup.
    • Inter-eNodeB handover via the X2 interface.
    • Manages multiple cells (typically 3 sectors per site).

    MME (Mobility Management Entity) — the core network control-plane node.

    • Signalling: NAS (Non-Access Stratum) messages — attach, detach, tracking area updates, authentication.
    • Mobility: tracks UE location at the tracking-area (TA) granularity.
    • Bearer management: sets up and tears down EPS bearers.
    • Handover: coordinates S1-based handovers between MMEs.
    • Security: manages authentication with HSS via the S6a interface.

    S-GW (Serving Gateway) — the user-plane anchor.

    • Anchors the user plane for inter-eNodeB handovers — packets are forwarded through the S-GW during handover, so the UE's IP session stays alive.
    • Mobility within an MME pool: the S-GW serving the UE stays constant as the UE moves between eNodeBs.
    • Charging data: collects usage statistics.
    • Lawful interception: provides intercept capability.

    P-GW (Packet Data Network Gateway) — the gateway to external networks.

    • Allocates the UE's IP address (IPv4, IPv6, or dual-stack).
    • Anchors the UE's session to external PDNs (Internet, IMS, corporate).
    • QoS enforcement: applies bearer-level policies (QCI, ARP).
    • Charging: per-bearer traffic accounting.
    • Lawful interception: same as S-GW.
    • Acts as the policy enforcement point for PCRF (Policy and Charging Rules Function).

    HSS (Home Subscriber Server) — the master subscriber database.

    • Subscription data (APN, QoS profile, roaming restrictions).
    • Authentication vectors (like GSM's HLR/AuC combined).
    • Location: which MME is currently serving the UE.
    • Interfaced by MME via S6a.

    PCRF (Policy and Charging Rules Function) — policy server.

    • QoS policies (bearer-level).
    • Charging rules (online/offline).
    • Interfaced by P-GW via Gx.

    5.4.3 Interfaces¶

    Interface Between Purpose
    Uu UE ↔ eNodeB Radio air interface
    S1-MME eNodeB ↔ MME Control plane (S1 for control)
    S1-U eNodeB ↔ S-GW User plane (S1 for user)
    X2 eNodeB ↔ eNodeB Inter-eNodeB handover and CoMP
    S5/S8 S-GW ↔ P-GW Within PLMN / between PLMNs
    S6a MME ↔ HSS Subscription and authentication
    S11 MME ↔ S-GW Control-plane within EPC
    Gx P-GW ↔ PCRF Policy and charging
    SGi P-GW ↔ PDN External network gateway

    5.4.4 The EPS bearer¶

    The EPS bearer is the basic unit of QoS in LTE. Each UE has one or more bearers:

    • Default bearer — always present; established at UE attach; best-effort.
    • Dedicated bearer — established on demand for specific traffic (e.g., VoLTE voice, video); has a specific QCI (QoS Class Identifier).
    QCI Service Priority Delay budget
    1 Conversational voice (VoLTE) 2 100 ms
    2 Conversational video 4 150 ms
    5 IMS signalling 1 100 ms
    7 Voice, video, gaming 7 100 ms
    8 TCP-based (web, email) 8 300 ms
    9 Best-effort 9 300 ms

    Each QCI maps to specific QoS parameters — scheduling priority, target delay, target error rate — which the eNodeB uses to prioritize over the air.

    Worked example 5.4 — LTE latency budget¶

    User-plane latency:

    Component Latency
    UE processing (encoder + ciphering + modulation) 2 ms
    Frame alignment (waiting for next subframe) 0.5 ms
    Air interface (HARQ + decoding) 4–8 ms
    eNodeB processing 1 ms
    S1-U transport (eNodeB to S-GW) 1–2 ms
    S-GW processing 0.5 ms
    P-GW processing (if intranet) 1 ms
    Total (one-way) ~10 ms

    Control-plane latency (idle → connected):

    • UE sends RRC connection request (random access preamble + message 3).
    • eNodeB → MME Initial UE Message.
    • MME authentication (via HSS, ~2 ms).
    • Bearer setup (MME → S-GW → eNodeB).
    • RRC reconfiguration to UE.
    • Total: ~50–100 ms for the first connection, ~10–20 ms for subsequent.

    Comparison with 3G: UMTS control-plane latency was ~200–500 ms. LTE's flat architecture cuts this by 5×.

    5.5 LTE physical layer details¶

    5.5.1 Frame structure¶

    FDD (Frequency Division Duplex): separate uplink and downlink frequencies.

    • Frame type 1: 10 ms radio frame; 20 slots; 2 slots per subframe.
    • Downlink and uplink frames are transmitted simultaneously on paired bands.

    TDD (Time Division Duplex): shared frequency, alternated in time.

    • Frame type 2: 10 ms radio frame; configurable uplink/downlink pattern.
    • Special subframe for the downlink-to-uplink switch (contains DwPTS, GP, UpPTS).

    Configurations (TDD): 7 UL/DL patterns, ranging from 1:9 (DL-heavy) to 5:3 (UL-heavy) to symmetric.

    5.5.2 Downlink physical channels¶

    Channel Purpose
    PDSCH (Physical Downlink Shared CHannel) Main data channel — user data + RRC signalling
    PDCCH (Physical Downlink Control CHannel) Scheduling grants, HARQ control, power control
    PHICH (Physical HARQ Indicator CHannel) HARQ ACK/NACK
    PCFICH (Physical Control Format Indicator CHannel) Size of PDCCH region
    PBCH (Physical Broadcast CHannel) System information (MIB — Master Info Block)
    PMCH (Physical Multicast CHannel) MBMS broadcast

    SSS (Secondary Synchronization Signal) Cell group ID and frame timing

    5.5.3 Uplink physical channels¶

    Channel Purpose
    PUSCH (Physical Uplink Shared CHannel) Main data channel
    PUCCH (Physical Uplink Control CHannel) UCI — HARQ ACK/NACK, CQI, scheduling requests
    PRACH (Physical Random Access CHannel) Random access preamble for initial access, scheduling requests, handover

    5.5.4 Link adaptation and HARQ¶

    Adaptive Modulation and Coding (AMC) — the eNodeB chooses modulation and coding rate based on CQI (Channel Quality Indicator) feedback:

    CQI Modulation Coding rate Efficiency (bps/Hz)
    1 QPSK 0.076 0.15
    4 QPSK 0.301 0.60
    7 16-QAM 0.490 1.96
    10 64-QAM 0.650 3.90
    13 64-QAM 0.820 4.92
    15 64-QAM 0.926 5.55

    The UE measures CSI and reports the CQI it can decode at 10% BLER. The eNodeB picks the modulation and coding accordingly.

    HARQ — as in UMTS, but even faster:

    • Fast retransmission — 8 ms round trip (1 ms subframe + 7 ms decode time).
    • Incremental redundancy — retransmissions carry new parity bits (chase combining for the simplest case).
    • Up to 4 HARQ processes in the downlink (parallel pipes) to keep the pipe full while retransmissions are pending.

    5.5.5 Reference signals and channel estimation¶

    Downlink: Cell-specific reference signals (CRS) are inserted every 6 subcarriers. The UE estimates the channel response by comparing received CRS to known CRS values.

    Uplink: Demodulation reference signals (DM-RS) embedded in PUSCH; sounding reference signals (SRS) for wideband channel sounding.

    The reference signals consume ~5–10% of resource elements — a necessary overhead for coherent detection.

    5.6 LTE-Advanced (Release 10+)¶

    Release 8 LTE met most IMT-Advanced requirements, but "true 4G" needed further evolution to meet the 1 Gbps peak-rate target. LTE-Advanced (Release 10) added:

    5.6.1 Carrier aggregation (CA)¶

    Combine multiple LTE carriers into one logical channel:

    \[ \boxed{\;B_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=1}^{K} B_i,\quad K \le 5\ \text{component carriers}\;} \]

    Options:

    • Intra-band contiguous — adjacent carriers in one band.
    • Intra-band non-contiguous — separate carriers in same band.
    • Inter-band — carriers in different bands (e.g., 800 MHz + 1800 MHz).

    Maximum: 5 carriers of 20 MHz = 100 MHz total → up to 3 Gbps peak (4×4 MIMO, 100 MHz, 64-QAM).

    Deployment impact: allows operators with fragmented spectrum (common in most markets) to offer wider "virtual" channels.

    5.6.2 Enhanced MIMO¶

    • Downlink: 8×8 MIMO (8 layers) → 8× peak rate.
    • Uplink: 4×4 MIMO.

    Peak rate with 8×8 MIMO + 20 MHz (no CA): 8 × 150 Mbps ≈ 1.2 Gbps.

    With CA + 8×8 MIMO: 5 carriers × 8 streams × 150 Mbps = 6 Gbps.

    5.6.3 Coordinated Multipoint (CoMP)¶

    Multiple eNodeBs coordinate to serve a UE at the cell edge:

    • Joint transmission (JT) — multiple eNodeBs transmit the same data to the UE, which combines them. Requires tight timing sync and CSI sharing.
    • Dynamic point selection (DPS) — the UE is served by the best eNodeB at any given moment (fast switching).
    • Coordinated scheduling/beamforming (CS/CB) — eNodeBs coordinate their beams to reduce interference.

    Benefit: cell-edge throughput improves by ~30–50% — significant because cell edge is where users experience the worst performance.

    Challenge: requires X2 interface backhaul with sub-1 ms latency and high bandwidth — expensive to deploy. CoMP is deployed in limited form in real networks.

    5.6.4 Relay nodes¶

    Small low-power base stations that forward data from a macro eNodeB to UEs:

    • Decode-and-forward relay — receives, decodes, re-encodes, transmits.
    • Amplify-and-forward relay — simple analog amplification (lower latency, more noise).

    Benefit: extend coverage to areas with weak macro coverage (rural, indoor) without full backhaul (relay uses the same spectrum for the backhaul, not fiber).

    5.6.5 Peak rates table¶

    Release Downlink peak Uplink peak Key features
    8 (LTE) 150 Mbps 75 Mbps 20 MHz, 2×2 MIMO, 64-QAM
    10 (LTE-A) 1 Gbps 500 Mbps 100 MHz (5×CA), 8×8 MIMO
    12 (LTE-A Pro) 3 Gbps 1.5 Gbps Enhanced CA, 256-QAM DL
    13 3.9 Gbps 1.5 Gbps 32-carrier CA (unlicensed)
    14 4.5 Gbps 2.2 Gbps eLAA, feLAA

    The path from Release 8 to Release 14 shows how 4G kept evolving — 30× the peak rate over 8 years.

    Worked example 5.5 — LTE-A carrier aggregation peak rate¶

    Scenario: 5 carriers of 20 MHz each, intra-band contiguous. 4×4 MIMO on each carrier. 64-QAM, rate-3/4 coding.

    Per carrier, per stream: ~100 Mbps (from Worked example 5.1, without MIMO's doubling).

    4 streams per carrier: 400 Mbps per carrier.

    5 carriers: 5 × 400 = 2 Gbps peak.

    With 64-QAM (6 bits/symbol) and 8×8 MIMO (8 streams): 5 × 8 × 100 = 4 Gbps — matching the Release 10 specification of ~3 Gbps (the difference is due to control overhead and reference signals).

    In practice: commercial LTE-A networks achieve 300–600 Mbps peak (with 60 MHz of aggregated spectrum), and 100–200 Mbps average in dense urban.

    5.7 WiMAX¶

    WiMAX (Worldwide Interoperability for Microwave Access) is the IEEE 802.16 family of standards for wireless metropolitan area networks. It was the "other" 4G standard — technically comparable to LTE, commercially eclipsed by it.

    5.7.1 IEEE 802.16 family¶

    Standard Year Purpose
    802.16 2001 Fixed wireless (line-of-sight, 10–66 GHz)
    802.16a 2003 Fixed wireless, 2–11 GHz (NLOS)
    802.16d 2004 Fixed WiMAX (OFDM, 256 FFT)
    802.16e 2005 Mobile WiMAX (OFDMA, 2048 FFT, scalable)
    802.16m 2011 WiMAX 2 (WirelessMAN-Advanced, ITU-R IMT-Advanced)

    Mobile WiMAX (802.16e) was the commercially relevant version — deployed in Korea (WiBro), USA (Sprint/Clearwire), Russia (Yota), Pakistan, and a few others.

    5.7.2 Mobile WiMAX physical layer¶

    OFDMA-based — very similar to LTE:

    Parameter WiMAX (802.16e) LTE (Release 8)
    Multiple access (DL/UL) OFDMA / OFDMA OFDMA / SC-FDMA
    Subcarrier spacing 10.94 kHz (fixed) 15 kHz
    FFT size 128, 512, 1024, 2048 128, 256, 512, 1024, 2048
    Channel bandwidth 1.25, 5, 10, 20 MHz 1.4, 3, 5, 10, 15, 20 MHz
    Frame duration 5 ms 10 ms
    Cyclic prefix 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32 Normal (4.7 μs), extended (16.67 μs)
    MIMO 2×2 (baseline), 4×4 (later) 2×2, 4×4, 8×8
    Modulation QPSK, 16-QAM, 64-QAM QPSK, 16-QAM, 64-QAM
    Duplex TDD (FDD optional) FDD or TDD

    Key difference: WiMAX uses OFDMA in the uplink too (rather than LTE's SC-FDMA). This gives WiMAX better frequency-scheduling flexibility but worse PAPR — a UE power-efficiency penalty.

    Another key difference: WiMAX's TDD-first design (FDD was optional) versus LTE's FDD-first design. TDD gives better uplink/downlink flexibility for data-heavy traffic but requires global time synchronization.

    5.7.3 WiMAX architecture¶

    ASN (Access Service Network) — the radio access network.

    • BS (Base Station) — the WiMAX base station.
    • ASN-GW (ASN Gateway) — controls one or more BSs, analogous to LTE's MME
    • S-GW combined.

    CSN (Connectivity Service Network) — the core network.

    • HA (Home Agent) — Mobile IP home agent (for mobility).
    • AAA — Authentication, Authorization, Accounting (RADIUS/Diameter).
    • DHCP — IP address allocation.
    • PCRF — policy control (in WiMAX 2).

    Key architectural difference from LTE: WiMAX has two core network nodes (BS + ASN-GW), vs. LTE's three (eNodeB + MME + S-GW/P-GW). LTE is flatter.

    5.7.4 WiMAX capacity¶

    WiMAX 802.16e peak rate (20 MHz, 2×2 MIMO, 64-QAM):

    • Downlink: ~75 Mbps (TDD, 3:1 DL/UL ratio).
    • Uplink: ~25 Mbps.

    With 802.16m (WiMAX 2), 4×4 MIMO, and 100 MHz (via aggregation):

    • Downlink: ~1 Gbps peak.

    In practice, Mobile WiMAX deployments achieved:

    • Peak: 20–40 Mbps DL per sector in well-deployed networks.
    • Average: 3–10 Mbps DL per user in loaded conditions.
    • Cell capacity: ~30–50 Mbps per sector (comparable to early LTE).

    Worked example 5.6 — WiMAX capacity per MHz per km²¶

    Scenario: WiMAX 802.16e, TDD, 20 MHz channel, 3:1 DL/UL ratio, 2×2 MIMO. Sector throughput (DL) = 30 Mbps. Site has 3 sectors → 90 Mbps per site.

    Cell area: With 2 km radius (typical suburban), each hexagonal cell has area ≈ \(2.598 \times R^2\) = 10.4 km². But 3 sites cover 3 cells, so per-cell area ≈ 10.4 km².

    Capacity per site per MHz per km²:

    \[ \frac{90\ \text{Mbps}}{20\ \text{MHz} \times 10.4\ \text{km}^2} \approx 0.43\ \text{bps/Hz/km}^2 \]

    Comparison with early LTE: LTE at 20 MHz, 2×2 MIMO, 3-sector, 2 km cells achieved ~150 Mbps per site → 0.72 bps/Hz/km² — roughly 1.7× WiMAX. LTE's better uplink (SC-FDMA) and higher MIMO order are the main reasons.

    PHY OFDMA (DL + UL) OFDMA (DL) + SC-FDMA (UL)
    Duplex TDD first, FDD optional FDD first, TDD optional
    Peak rate ~75 Mbps (Rel 1), ~1 Gbps (Rel 2) 150 Mbps (Rel 8), 1 Gbps (Rel 10)

    5.8.1 Why WiMAX lost¶

    WiMAX lost for reasons outside the technical specs:

    1. Ecosystem. LTE inherited the global GSM/UMTS operator base — 200+ operators worldwide, each with existing spectrum and customers. WiMAX operators had to build networks and customer bases from scratch.

    2. Backward compatibility. LTE devices are dual-mode with 2G/3G → users can drop out of coverage gracefully. WiMAX devices only work on WiMAX.

    3. Standards process. 3GPP has a clear, predictable release cycle (new release every 12–18 months) with broad industry participation. IEEE 802.16 was less cohesive and slower to iterate.

    4. Device ecosystem. Qualcomm, Apple, Samsung, etc. built LTE chipsets — not WiMAX. By 2013, LTE handsets were everywhere; WiMAX handsets were scarce.

    5. Intel's exit. Intel (the driving force behind WiMAX) shut down its WiMAX division in 2010 and pivoted to LTE — a fatal blow.

    6. The TDD vs. FDD issue. WiMAX was TDD-first; most operators owned FDD spectrum (from 2G/3G). Using WiMAX meant not using their existing spectrum.

    Technically, WiMAX was comparable to or in some ways slightly ahead of LTE (especially early — WiMAX supported 4×4 MIMO before LTE did). Commercially, it lost decisively to the much larger LTE ecosystem.

    5.8.2 Legacy of WiMAX¶

    Even though WiMAX lost as a cellular standard, its technology influenced:

    • LTE and 5G NR PHY — WiMAX proved OFDMA + MIMO at scale, which LTE adopted.
    • WiFi (802.11) — WiMAX's OFDMA influenced WiFi 4/5/6/7 (802.11n/ac/ax/be).
    • Fixed wireless access (FWA) — WiMAX lives on as a niche FWA technology; 5G FWA (using mmWave) is essentially its spiritual successor.

    5.9 Radio and network components¶

    Modern 4G/5G networks use advanced radio and network infrastructure that goes beyond the "macro base station" model. Understanding these components is essential for network design.

    5.9.1 Antenna systems¶

    MIMO antenna arrays:

    • Co-located MIMO — multiple antennas at one site, closely spaced.
    • Distributed MIMO — antennas spread across cells, coordinated (used in CoMP).

    Active Antenna Systems (AAS):

    • Traditional: passive antennas + remote radio unit (RRU) + feeder cable.
    • AAS: each antenna element has its own RF chain — enables massive MIMO (64–256 elements) and 3D beamforming (both horizontal and vertical).

    Massive MIMO (precursor to 5G):

    • 64+ antennas at the base station.
    • Beamforming gain: up to 10 log₁₀(64) = 18 dB per beam.
    • Multi-user MIMO: serves multiple UEs simultaneously.
    • Used in 4G LTE-A Pro and 5G NR.

    5.9.2 Remote Radio Head (RRH) and Baseband Unit (BBU)¶

    Traditional base station: RF and baseband in one box at the tower → heavy, hard to maintain, and the feeder cable (coax) wastes power.

    Modern split:

    • RRH (Remote Radio Head) — RF and antenna at the tower top. Small, light, power-efficient.
    • BBU (Baseband Unit) — baseband processing at the bottom of the tower or in a centralized location.
    • CPRI/OBSAI interface — fiber carrying digitized I/Q samples between RRH and BBU.

    Advantages:

    • Higher power efficiency — no coax feeder losses.
    • Easier installation — lighter tower-top equipment.
    • Fiber transport — the BBU can be far away (up to 20 km with CPRI).

    5.9.3 Cloud RAN (C-RAN)¶

    C-RAN centralizes BBUs from many cell sites into a BBU hotel:

    • RRHs at the cell sites (tower tops).
    • Dark fiber from each RRH to the centralized BBU pool.
    • BBU pool — many BBUs co-located in one facility.

    Advantages:

    • Statistical multiplexing — 100 BBUs can serve 300 cell sites, because each site's traffic varies throughout the day.
    • Easier upgrades — swap BBU pool hardware without visiting cell sites.
    • CoMP-ready — coordinated transmission is trivial when BBUs are co-located.
    • Power savings — centralize cooling and power supply.

    Disadvantages:

    • Fiber dependency — every cell site needs dark fiber (a significant cost).
    • Latency — CPRI latency must be < 100 μs for tight coordination.
    • Single point of failure — if the BBU pool fails, all cells go down (mitigated by redundancy).

    Adoption: Used in dense urban and 5G deployments; limited in rural areas where fiber is expensive.

    5.9.4 Small cells¶

    Small cells are low-power base stations for capacity and coverage in specific areas:

    Type Power Coverage Use
    Femtocell 10–100 mW 10–50 m Home/small office
    Picocell 100–500 mW 50–200 m Enterprise, hotspot
    Microcell 1–5 W 200–1000 m Dense urban
    Macrocell 20–40 W 1–30 km Wide-area coverage

    Heterogeneous network (HetNet) — mixing cell types to optimize coverage and capacity.

    5.9.5 Backhaul¶

    Backhaul connects cell sites to the core network. Options:

    Technology Capacity Typical distance Use
    Fiber 1–100 Gbps up to 80 km Preferred; where available
    Microwave 100 Mbps–1 Gbps 1–30 km (LOS) Rural, difficult-to-reach sites
    E-band (70/80 GHz) 1–10 Gbps 1–3 km Dense urban
    Free-space optical 1–10 Gbps 1–2 km Line-of-sight only
    Satellite 10–100 Mbps Global Remote sites
    DSL 10–100 Mbps 1–5 km Small cells (limited)

    Backhaul is the hidden bottleneck — a 4G site with 3 sectors × 300 Mbps peak needs ~1–3 Gbps of backhaul at peak. Fiber or microwave capacity often lags the radio capacity by 5–10 years, causing congestion at the cell.

    5G target: 10–20 Gbps per site → nearly all 5G sites need fiber.

    5.10 Network capacity and design¶

    5.10.1 Spectral efficiency¶

    Peak spectral efficiency (bps/Hz) is the theoretical maximum:

    System Peak DL spectral efficiency Peak UL
    UMTS R99 0.4 bps/Hz 0.4 bps/Hz
    HSPA+ (R11) 8.4 bps/Hz 2.3 bps/Hz
    LTE (Release 8) 7.5 bps/Hz (2×2) 3.75 bps/Hz (1×2)
    LTE-Advanced (Release 10) 15 bps/Hz (8×8) 6.75 bps/Hz (4×4)
    LTE-A Pro (Release 13) 25 bps/Hz (massive MIMO) 9.6 bps/Hz
    5G NR (Sub-6) 30 bps/Hz 15 bps/Hz
    5G NR (mmWave) 60+ bps/Hz 30 bps/Hz

    Peak is achievable only in ideal conditions — highest SNR, best MIMO rank, no interference. Average spectral efficiency in real networks is ~30–50% of peak.

    5.10.2 Cell-edge performance¶

    The cell-edge user is the one who experiences the worst signal — typically at the cell boundary, with interference from neighboring cells at full strength.

    Metrics:

    • 5th percentile throughput — the throughput achieved by the slowest 5% of users.
    • Cell-edge spectral efficiency — bps/Hz at the 5th percentile.
    System Average spectral efficiency Cell-edge spectral efficiency
    UMTS R99 0.5 bps/Hz/cell 0.03 bps/Hz/cell
    HSPA+ 2 bps/Hz/cell 0.15 bps/Hz/cell
    LTE (2×2) 3 bps/Hz/cell 0.1 bps/Hz/cell
    LTE-A (8×8 + CoMP) 6 bps/Hz/cell 0.2 bps/Hz/cell
    5G NR (massive MIMO) 15 bps/Hz/cell 0.3 bps/Hz/cell

    Cell-edge is 10–100× worse than average — this is the fundamental challenge of cellular design. CoMP, beamforming, and small cells all target cell-edge improvement.

    Worked example 5.7 — capacity per km² for LTE¶

    Scenario: LTE Release 8, 20 MHz, 3-sector macro site, average spectral efficiency 2.5 bps/Hz/cell (average load).

    Cell throughput: 2.5 bps/Hz × 20 MHz = 50 Mbps per cell. Per 3-sector site: 150 Mbps.

    Site coverage area: In suburban deployment with 2 km cell radius:

    • Cell area ≈ \(2.598 R^2\) = 10.4 km² per cell.
    • 3 sectors per site = 3 cells.
    • Site coverage = 3 × 10.4 = 31.2 km².

    Capacity per km²: 150 Mbps / 31.2 km² ≈ 4.8 Mbps/km².

    With LTE-Advanced (8×8 MIMO, CA, ~2× spectral efficiency): ~10 Mbps/km².

    With 5G NR (mmWave, dense small cells, ~10× spectral efficiency): 50–100 Mbps/km² in dense urban — the reason 5G is being deployed mainly in dense areas.

    5.10.3 Traffic density and capacity planning¶

    Traffic density (Gbps/km²) is the key design metric in modern networks:

    Scenario Traffic density Cell density needed
    Rural 0.01–0.1 Gbps/km² Macro cells (10–30 km)
    Suburban 0.1–1 Gbps/km² Macro cells (1–3 km)
    Urban 1–10 Gbps/km² Macro + microcells (500 m)
    Dense urban 10–50 Gbps/km² Microcells + picocells (200–500 m)
    Ultra-dense 50–300 Gbps/km² Small cells + DAS (50–200 m)

    Network densification — deploying more cells per km² — is the main way to increase capacity. But each cell needs backhaul, power, and site costs → the cost per Gbps grows nonlinearly as cell size shrinks.

    5G mmWave enables very dense deployment (10+ cells/km²) at high cost — viable only in stadiums, airports, and dense commercial areas.

    5.11 Chapter summary¶

    1. 4G was defined by IMT-Advanced requirements: 1 Gbps peak downlink, 100 Mbps high-mobility, all-IP, low latency. LTE and WiMAX both met these — LTE won the market for ecosystem reasons.
    2. OFDMA (downlink) and SC-FDMA (uplink) replace WCDMA — OFDMA for flexibility and MIMO compatibility, SC-FDMA for UE power efficiency.
    3. LTE resource blocks are 12 subcarriers × 7 symbols × 2 slots; 100 RBs in 20 MHz; each RB 180 kHz wide, 0.5 ms long. Subcarrier spacing is 15 kHz.
    4. MIMO provides spatial multiplexing (linear capacity growth with \(\min(N_t, N_r)\)), transmit diversity (reliability), and beamforming (coverage). This is the workhorse behind 100+ Mbps per 20 MHz.
    5. The EPC (Evolved Packet Core) is flat, all-IP: eNodeB → S-GW → P-GW → Internet, with MME and HSS for control. Latency < 10 ms vs. 50 ms in UMTS.
    6. The eNodeB combines RNC + NodeB functions; X2 interface enables inter-eNodeB handover without going through the core.
    7. LTE-Advanced adds carrier aggregation (up to 5 carriers, 100 MHz), higher-order MIMO (8×8), CoMP, and relays — pushing peak to 3 Gbps.
    8. WiMAX was technically comparable to LTE but lost commercially because of ecosystem, backward compatibility, and market dynamics. Its technology influenced LTE, WiFi, and 5G NR.
    9. Radio and network components — RRH, BBU, C-RAN, small cells, massive MIMO — are what make modern 4G/5G deployments possible. Backhaul is often the hidden bottleneck.
    10. Network capacity depends on spectral efficiency, cell-edge performance, and cell density. Cell-edge is 10–100× worse than average — that's the fundamental cellular challenge.
    11. Peak spectral efficiency grew from 0.4 bps/Hz (UMTS) to 60+ bps/Hz (5G mmWave). Average grew from ~0.5 to ~15 bps/Hz/cell — still 10× below peak, and cell-edge 10–100× below average.

      15 bps/Hz. WiMAX 802.16m meets them via 100 MHz aggregation, 4×4 MIMO, and advanced relaying → 1 Gbps peak. Both standards meet the requirements technically; LTE won commercially.

    2. Downlink OFDMA: the eNodeB has abundant power (mains-powered, no battery) and flexible frequency-scheduling needs → OFDMA's higher PAPR (10–12 dB) is acceptable, and its spectral flexibility is valuable. Uplink SC-FDMA: the UE is battery-powered — OFDMA's PAPR would require 10 dB PA backoff → inefficient → short battery + poor coverage. SC-FDMA's DFT-precoding reduces PAPR to 3–6 dB → the UE PA operates near saturation → +6 dB uplink power → ~2× cell radius. Trade-off: slightly lower spectral efficiency in the uplink (must allocate contiguous RBs), but coverage and battery life win.
    3. LTE resource block: 12 subcarriers × 180 kHz bandwidth × 7 symbols × 0.5 ms slot (normal CP). Time-frequency unit = 84 resource elements (REs). In 20 MHz: 100 RBs (20 MHz / 180 kHz ≈ 111, but guard band leaves 100 usable). Per subframe (1 ms): 100 RBs × 168 REs = 16,800 REs total, minus reference signals and control → ~100 RBs worth of data.
    4. Spatial multiplexing: multiple independent streams → linear capacity growth. Used when channel is high-rank (rich scattering, good SNR). Transmit diversity: same data duplicated across antennas with orthogonal codes (Alamouti/SFBC) → 3 dB gain in Rayleigh fading at half the rate. Used for control channels, cell edge, and low-SNR UEs. Beamforming: focus energy toward a specific UE → 10 log(N_t) dB gain. Used for coverage extension, interference reduction, and MU-MIMO.
    5. LTE EPC architecture: (a) eNodeB — base station, all radio functions + RRC. (b) MME — control plane, NAS, authentication, mobility, bearer management. (c) S-GW — user-plane anchor for handovers, charging. (d) P-GW — gateway to external PDN, IP allocation, QoS. (e) HSS — subscription database + authentication. Interfaces: Uu (UE-eNodeB), S1-MME (eNodeB-MME), S1-U (eNodeB-S-GW), S5/S8 (S-GW-P-GW), S6a (MME-HSS), S11 (MME-S-GW), X2 (eNodeB-eNodeB).
    6. Flat architecture: UMTS has NodeB → RNC → SGSN/MSC — three levels of signalling, each adding latency. LTE collapses NodeB+RNC into eNodeB and uses direct S1 to the core → two levels. Result: user-plane RTT drops from ~50 ms to ~10 ms; control-plane (idle→connected) from ~500 ms to ~50–100 ms. This was critical for interactive services (gaming, VoLTE, industrial IoT) that were impossible on 3G.
    7. LTE-A enhancements: (a) Carrier aggregation — up to 5 carriers × 20 MHz = 100 MHz → 5× rate → up to 1 Gbps with 2×2 MIMO. (b) Higher-order MIMO — 8×8 DL → 8× rate → up to 3 Gbps with CA. (c) CoMP — coordinated transmission from multiple eNodeBs improves cell-edge throughput 30–50%. (d) Relays — decode-and-forward or amplify-and-forward relays extend coverage to remote areas without new fiber backhaul.
    8. WiMAX architecture: (a) ASN — BS + ASN-GW. (b) CSN — HA (mobile IP), AAA, DHCP. Compare to LTE: WiMAX has 2 nodes (BS+ASN-GW) where LTE has 3 (eNodeB+MME+S/P-GW) — LTE is flatter. Why WiMAX lost: (i) ecosystem — LTE had 200+ operator backing, WiMAX had ~30; (ii) backward compatibility — LTE dual-mode with 2G/3G, WiMAX not; (iii) Intel's exit (2010) was fatal; (iv) TDD-first design didn't match most operators' FDD spectrum; (v) slower standards iteration.
    9. C-RAN: centralizes BBUs from many cell sites into a BBU pool; RRHs at cell sites; fiber (CPRI) carries digitized samples. Advantages: statistical multiplexing of BBU processing, easier upgrades, CoMP-ready, power savings. Challenges: fiber everywhere (huge cost), CPRI latency budget (< 100 μs), single point of failure at the BBU pool (needs redundancy), and the recent trend toward distributed/virtualized RAN (O-RAN) has shifted industry views.
    10. Spectral efficiency per cell: LTE ~3 bps/Hz/cell average (DL, 2×2 MIMO, 20 MHz). LTE-A ~6 bps/Hz/cell with 8×8 + CoMP. Per km²: LTE 50 Mbps/cell ÷ 10 km² cell ≈ 5 Mbps/km². LTE-A 100 Mbps/cell ÷ 10 km² = 10 Mbps/km². Cell-edge is worse because: at the cell boundary, the serving signal is weakest (max path loss) while interfering signals from adjacent cells are strongest (min path loss from neighbors) → SIR is worst. This yields 10–100× worse throughput. CoMP, beamforming, and small cells target this.
    11. LTE vs. LTE-A vs. 5G NR: (a) LTE — 150 Mbps peak, 2×2 MIMO, 20 MHz, ~10 ms latency, ~3 bps/Hz/cell average. (b) LTE-A — 3 Gbps peak, 8×8 MIMO, 100 MHz CA, ~10 ms latency, ~6 bps/Hz/cell average. (c) 5G NR — 20 Gbps peak (mmWave), 64–256 antenna massive MIMO, 400 MHz per carrier (mmWave), ~1 ms latency, ~15 bps/Hz/cell average (Sub-6), 30 bps/Hz/cell (mmWave).
    12. Calculation: (a) 2×2 MIMO: per carrier ~150 Mbps peak × 3 carriers = 450 Mbps — well exceeds 100 Mbps target. (b) 4×4 MIMO: ~300 Mbps per carrier × 3 = 900 Mbps. (c) 8×8 MIMO: ~600 Mbps per carrier × 3 = 1.8 Gbps. Answer: even 2×2 MIMO with 3× CA achieves 450 Mbps — easily satisfies 100 Mbps target. For a high-traffic cell, 4×4 MIMO gives 900 Mbps peak with room for multiple users at once.

    Suggested tutorial problems¶

    1. IMT-Advanced check. For each requirement (peak DL, peak UL, spectral efficiency, latency), compute whether LTE-Advanced (Release 10) meets it. Show calculations and cite the standard's claims.

    2. Resource block count. Verify that 20 MHz LTE has 100 RBs. Show the arithmetic: 20 MHz / 180 kHz = 111.11. Where do the 11 missing RBs go?

    3. Peak throughput. Compute peak LTE throughput for: (a) 10 MHz, 2×2 MIMO, 64-QAM, rate-3/4; (b) 20 MHz, 4×4 MIMO, 64-QAM, rate-3/4; (c) 60 MHz (3×CA), 4×4 MIMO, 64-QAM, rate-3/4.

    4. SC-FDMA vs OFDMA. For a UE with 23 dBm PA and maximum PAPR limits: OFDMA allows 14 dBm average TX (10 dB backoff); SC-FDMA allows 19 dBm (4 dB backoff). Compute the cell radius for each, using path-loss exponent 3.5 and equal receiver sensitivity. Which is preferred and why?

    5. MIMO modes. A UE is at 10 dB SNR with rich scattering — which MIMO mode should the eNodeB choose? At 0 dB SNR with one dominant path — which mode? Justify.

    6. EPC latency. Trace the path of a packet from a UE to a Google server. List each network element and its contribution to latency. Total one-way and RTT.

    7. Carrier aggregation. Design an LTE-A configuration for an operator with spectrum at 800 MHz (10 MHz), 1800 MHz (20 MHz), and 2600 MHz (20 MHz). How many component carriers? What peak downlink rate with 4×4 MIMO? Note any deployment challenges (inter-band CA, antenna sharing).

    8. WiMAX capacity. For WiMAX 802.16e, 10 MHz TDD (3:1 DL/UL), 2×2 MIMO: compute peak DL and UL. Then compute the site capacity (3-sector) assuming 50% of peak average.

    9. Cell-edge improvement. A cell has average throughput 50 Mbps, cell-edge (5th percentile) 2 Mbps. CoMP provides a 3 dB SIR improvement at cell edge, translating to a 2× throughput improvement. Compute the new cell-edge throughput. What is the % of users below 1 Mbps before and after?

    10. Massive MIMO gain. A 64-antenna massive MIMO system beams to 8 UEs simultaneously. Compute the per-user SNR gain vs. a single antenna (10 log(64/8) = 9 dB per user). Compute the total capacity gain vs. 2×2 MIMO.

    11. Backhaul. A 4G LTE site has 3 sectors, each 20 MHz, 4×4 MIMO. Peak per sector is 600 Mbps. What fiber backhaul capacity does the site need at peak? At 60% average load?

    12. 5G vs 4G. Compare 5G NR (Sub-6, 100 MHz, massive MIMO) with LTE-A (20 MHz × 3, 4×4 MIMO) on: peak rate, average rate, latency, and capacity per km². What are the main reasons for 5G's advantage?

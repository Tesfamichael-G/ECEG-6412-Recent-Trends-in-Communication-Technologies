    Chapter 6 — Next Generation Networks (NGN)¶

    Chapter at a glance

    Contact hours: 3 lecture · Home study: 7 h · Part IV — Emerging technologies

    Coverage (syllabus): overview of next generation networks · personal area networks (Bluetooth and ZigBee) · wireless data services (WiFi) · UWB · 5G mobile network technology and the Internet of Things (IoT).

    Learning outcomes¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

    1. Explain what Next Generation Networks (NGN) means and how the concept evolved from all-IP convergence to the IoT era.
    2. Describe Bluetooth Classic and BLE — piconet topology, protocols, data rates, and power profiles — and choose between them for a given application.
    3. Analyse ZigBee — mesh topology, IEEE 802.15.4 PHY/MAC, AODV routing, and why it dominates low-power sensor networks.
    4. Compare the WiFi standards (802.11a/b/g/n/ac/ax/be) — rates, bands, MIMO, OFDMA — and select a WiFi generation for a given deployment.
    5. Explain UWB — impulse radio, timing, ranging — and its role in high-precision positioning.
    6. Describe LPWAN technologies (LoRa, NB-IoT, Sigfox) — range, data rate, battery life — and their role in IoT connectivity.
    7. Explain 5G network architecture — service-based core, network slicing, edge computing — and the three 5G use cases (eMBB, URLLC, mMTC).
    8. Compute IoT device battery life, coverage range, and network capacity for typical LPWAN/WiFi/BLE deployments.
    9. Design a connectivity strategy — WPAN, WLAN, LPWAN, cellular — for a real-world IoT application based on range, power, and data requirements.

    6.1 What is an NGN?¶

    6.1.1 From convergence to everything-over-IP¶

    The term Next Generation Network (NGN) was defined by the ITU-T in the early 2000s as a packet-based network able to provide telecommunication services — voice, data, video — over a single IP infrastructure with guaranteed QoS.

    The original NGN vision (2004 ITU-T Y.2001):

    "A packet-based network able to provide telecommunication services and able to make use of multiple broadband, QoS-enabled transport technologies, in which service-related functions are independent from underlying transport-related technologies."

    Key NGN principles:

    • Packet-based — everything rides on IP, not circuit switching.
    • Service / transport separation — services are decoupled from the underlying network via open APIs.
    • QoS-enabled — unlike the best-effort internet, NGN guarantees performance.
    • Any-access — fixed, mobile, wireless — all converge to one network.
    • Multi-service — voice, data, video, IoT over one infrastructure.

    6.1.2 The stages of NGN evolution¶

    Stage 1 (2000–2010): All-IP convergence.

    • Replace PSTN circuit switching with IP-based voice (VoIP).
    • Replace 2G/3G core networks with IP (R4 MSC Server + MGW → all-IP EPC in LTE).
    • Example: BT's 21CN project (UK), Verizon's FiOS (fiber to the home).
    • Key technologies: SIP, MPLS, IP Multimedia Subsystem (IMS).

    Stage 2 (2010–2020): Fixed-mobile convergence (FMC).

    • Unify fixed and mobile access under one core (EPC → 5G SBA).
    • Voice over WiFi (VoWiFi), LTE-WiFi aggregation (LWA).
    • Example: T-Mobile's VoWiFi, AT&T's small-cell deployment.
    • Key technologies: ANDSF, ePDG, 5G NSA/SA.

    Stage 3 (2020s): IoT convergence.

    • Billions of devices — sensors, actuators, cameras — all IP-connected.
    • LPWAN (LoRa, NB-IoT, Sigfox) sits alongside WiFi and cellular for IoT.
    • 5G and beyond: network slicing, edge computing, massive machine-type communication.
    • Key technologies: MQTT, CoAP, 5G core, edge computing, ML.

    The NGN concept broadened from "voice over IP" to "everything over IP" — the network becomes a platform for all digital services.

    6.1.3 The NGN landscape in one picture¶

    Category Standard Range Data rate Power
    WPAN (personal) Bluetooth Classic 10–100 m 1–3 Mbps Low
    Bluetooth LE 10–100 m 125 kbps–2 Mbps Very low
    ZigBee 10–100 m 250 kbps Very low
    UWB 1–10 m 100 Mbps–1 Gbps Low
    WLAN (local) WiFi 4/5/6/7 30–100 m 100 Mbps–30 Gbps High
    WMAN (metropolitan) WiMAX, 5G FWA 1–50 km 100 Mbps–1 Gbps High
    WWAN (wide) 4G LTE 1–30 km 100 Mbps–1 Gbps High
    5G NR 100 m–10 km 1–20 Gbps High
    LPWAN (IoT) LoRa 2–15 km 0.3–37.5 kbps Extremely low
    NB-IoT 1–10 km 26–127 kbps Very low
    Sigfox 3–50 km 100 bps Extremely low
    Satellite GEO, LEO global 1–200 Mbps —

    Each row is a different trade-off between range, rate, and power. The NGN is about making all of them work together — not replacing them.

    6.2 Personal Area Networks — Bluetooth¶

    6.2.1 Bluetooth Classic (BR/EDR) — the original¶

    Bluetooth Classic (Basic Rate / Enhanced Data Rate, BR/EDR) was defined in 1994 by Ericsson and standardized as IEEE 802.15.1 in 2002. Designed as a cable replacement for short-range connections (headphones, mice, keyboards).

    Key parameters:

    Parameter Value
    Frequency 2.4 GHz ISM band (2.400–2.4835 GHz)
    Channel spacing 1 MHz; 79 channels (or 40 in low-energy variants)
    Modulation GFSK (Basic Rate), π/4-DQPSK, 8-DPSK (EDR)
    Data rate 1 Mbps (BR), 2–3 Mbps (EDR)
    Range Class 1: 100 m, Class 2: 10 m, Class 3: 1 m
    Tx power Class 1: 20 dBm, Class 2: 4 dBm, Class 3: 0 dBm
    Topology Piconet (1 master + up to 7 active slaves)

    The piconet:

    • One master device controls the piconet — it defines the hopping sequence, the slot timing, and the channel access rules.
    • Up to 7 active slaves simultaneously; more can be in parked or sniff mode (low-power standby).
    • Slots are 625 μs long; master and slave alternate transmissions.
    • Frequency hopping at 1600 hops/s (each slot on a different channel) — resist to interference and multi-path.

    Scatternets. Multiple piconets can be interconnected by a device that belongs to two or more piconets simultaneously. This is limited in practice (the shared device is a bottleneck).

    Protocol stack (simplified):

    • Radio → Baseband → LMP (Link Manager Protocol) → L2CAP (Logical Link Control and Adaptation) → RFCOMM (serial port) or A2DP (audio).
    • SDP (Service Discovery Protocol) — finds available services.
    • Profiles — application-level standards (HSP for headset, HFP for handsfree, A2DP for audio, HID for input devices, OPP for file transfer).

    Why frequency hopping? The 2.4 GHz ISM band is shared by WiFi, ZigBee, microwaves, and Bluetooth itself. Hopping across 79 channels means a narrowband interferer only affects 1/79 of slots — Bluetooth retransmits the failed slots on different frequencies. Effective interference resistance without needing a coordination protocol.

    6.2.2 Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) — the IoT version¶

    BLE (Bluetooth Smart) was introduced in Bluetooth 4.0 (2010) as a fundamentally different radio optimized for IoT sensors and wearables.

    Key design changes:

    Aspect Bluetooth Classic BLE
    Target use Continuous data (audio, files) Bursty sensor data
    Channel count 79 × 1 MHz 40 × 2 MHz (3 advertising + 37 data)
    Connection setup ~seconds ~3 ms
    Peak current (TX) 30–40 mA ~10 mA
    Standby current 100 μA – 1 mA < 1 μA
    Duty cycle Continuous Bursty (advertise → sleep)
    Data rate (peak) 2–3 Mbps (EDR) 1 Mbps (BT 4.0), 2 Mbps (BT 5.0)
    Range (BT 5.0) 10–100 m 10–400 m (with coded PHY)

    The BLE advertising model.

    BLE devices don't need a connection. A peripheral broadcasts advertisement packets on 3 advertising channels; a central scans for them, then either reads the advertisement data (broadcast mode) or requests a connection.

    Advertising interval — from 20 ms to 10.24 s. Longer intervals = lower power but higher latency.

    GATT (Generic Attribute Profile).

    BLE data is organized as services and characteristics: - Service — a group of related characteristics (e.g., "Heart Rate Service"). - Characteristic — a single data value (e.g., "Heart Rate Measurement") with properties (read, write, notify).

    Standard profiles (Heart Rate, Battery, Device Information) are defined by the Bluetooth SIG; custom services are common for specific applications.

    Bluetooth versions:

    Version Year Key additions
    4.0 2010 BLE introduced
    4.1 2013 IoT-friendly coexistence with LTE
    4.2 2014 IPv6/6LoWPAN support, faster
    5.0 2016 2× speed (2 Mbps), 4× range (400 m), 8× advertising payload
    5.1 2019 Direction finding (AoA/AoD) for indoor positioning
    5.2 2020 LE Audio (LC3 codec, multi-stream)
    5.3 2021 Periodic advertising, channel classification
    5.4 2023 PAwR (periodic advertising with responses)

    Worked example 6.1 — BLE battery life¶

    Scenario: A temperature sensor sends one 10-byte reading every 10 s. Advertising is not used (connection mode). Each transmission uses 2 ms at 10 mA; the MCU consumes 5 μA in sleep. Battery: 1000 mAh coin cell.

    Energy per transmission:

    • Transmit: 2 ms × 10 mA = 20 μAh per transmission.
    • Sleep between transmissions: (10 s − 2 ms) × 5 μA ≈ 50 μAh per interval.
    • Total per 10 s: ~70 μAh × (10 s / 3600 s/h) ... let me redo:
    • 2 ms at 10 mA = 20 mA·ms = 0.00556 mAh per transmission.
    • 9998 ms at 5 μA = 0.0000139 mAh per interval.
    • Per 10 s interval: 0.00557 mAh.
    • Per hour: 360 intervals × 0.00557 = 2.0 mAh/h.
    • Battery life: 1000 mAh / 2.0 mA ≈ 500 hours ≈ 21 days.

    Optimization: Increase advertising interval to 30 s and use a lower TX power:

    • Transmit: 2 ms × 5 mA = 0.00278 mAh per transmission.
    • Per hour: 120 intervals × 0.00278 = 0.334 mAh/h.
    • Battery life: 1000 / 0.334 ≈ 3000 hours ≈ 125 days (~4 months).

    Realistic for IoT: A properly tuned BLE sensor (event-driven, not periodic) can last 1–3 years on a coin cell. Longer life requires LPWAN (ZigBee, LoRa, NB-IoT).

    6.2.3 Bluetooth Classic vs. BLE — choosing¶

    Application Recommended
    Wireless headphones Bluetooth Classic (A2DP)
    Fitness tracker / smartwatch BLE
    Wireless mouse / keyboard BLE (or Classic HID)
    Continuous audio streaming Bluetooth Classic (higher rate)
    Hearing aids BLE (LE Audio, BT 5.2)
    Indoor location beacon BLE (with 5.1 direction finding)
    File transfer between phones Bluetooth Classic
    Medical sensor BLE

    Rule of thumb: if the data is continuous and needs a high rate → Classic. If it's bursty and power is critical → BLE.

    6.3 Personal Area Networks — ZigBee¶

    6.3.1 What is ZigBee?¶

    ZigBee (IEEE 802.15.4) is a low-power, low-data-rate, mesh-capable WPAN standard designed for sensor networks and home automation.

    Key parameters:

    Parameter Value
    Frequency 2.4 GHz (global, 16 channels), 868 MHz (EU), 915 MHz (US)
    Modulation OQPSK (2.4 GHz) or BPSK (sub-GHz)
    Data rate 250 kbps (2.4 GHz), 40 kbps (915 MHz), 20 kbps (868 MHz)
    Range 10–100 m per hop (typical); extended via mesh
    Topology Star, tree, or mesh
    Power (TX) ~30 mA
    Power (sleep) < 1 μA
    Network capacity 65,000+ nodes per network

    6.3.2 The ZigBee protocol stack¶

    Built on IEEE 802.15.4 (PHY + MAC), ZigBee adds:

    • NWK (Network) layer — mesh routing, address assignment, network formation.
    • APS (Application Support) layer — endpoint multiplexing, binding.
    • ZDO (ZigBee Device Object) — device discovery, service discovery.
    • Application profiles — standardized profiles (Home Automation 1.2, Smart Energy, Light Link) and manufacturer-specific profiles.

    Device types:

    • Coordinator — forms the network, assigns addresses, maintains the network. Exactly one per network.
    • Router — forwards traffic, participates in mesh. Typically mains-powered.
    • End Device — leaves/actuators/sensors, sleeps most of the time. Typically battery-powered.

    6.3.3 Mesh networking and AODV¶

    ZigBee supports mesh topologies — any router can forward for any other. The routing protocol is AODV (Ad hoc On-demand Distance Vector):

    1. The source broadcasts a Route Request (RREQ) — "who has the destination?"
    2. Intermediate routers forward the RREQ, adding their address.
    3. The destination receives the RREQ and sends back a Route Reply (RREP) — tracing the reverse path.
    4. Routers cache the route for future use.
    5. Route maintenance: if a link breaks, an RERR (Route Error) propagates back; source initiates a new RREQ.

    Why AODV? On-demand — no routing table is maintained proactively. For sensor networks (mostly idle), this saves energy.

    Worked example 6.2 — ZigBee mesh coverage¶

    Scenario: A factory wants to cover 500 m × 500 m with ZigBee sensors. Each sensor has range 30 m. Floor has walls reducing effective range to 20 m.

    Single-hop coverage: ~20 m.

    Mesh approach: deploy routers every 15 m (overlap for reliability).

    • Row spacing: 15 m; column spacing: 15 m.
    • Number of routers: (500/15) × (500/15) ≈ 34 × 34 = 1,156 routers.
    • This is more than ZigBee can handle reliably (practical limit ~500 routers per network).

    Alternative: hierarchical (cluster tree).

    • Divide area into 4 clusters of 250 × 250 m.
    • Each cluster has 1 coordinator + ~100 routers + many end devices.
    • Alternative: reduce coverage requirement — use fewer sensors, accept longer hops (multi-hop latency increases).

    The trade-off: more hops = larger area but higher latency and lower throughput (each hop halves bandwidth). For typical building automation, a single-cell 10,000 m² deployment with 200-300 devices is comfortable for ZigBee; larger deployments need multiple networks or gateway bridges.

    6.3.4 ZigBee vs. BLE¶

    Aspect ZigBee BLE
    Topology Mesh Star (BLE Mesh is a newer standard)
    Data rate 250 kbps 1–2 Mbps
    Range per hop 10–100 m 10–100 m
    Network size 65,000+ ~10–20 typical; higher with BLE Mesh
    Power (idle) < 1 μA < 1 μA
    Latency 15–30 ms typical 3 ms
    Standard IEEE 802.15.4 Bluetooth SIG
    Ecosystem Smart home (Philips Hue, SmartThings) Wearables, phones, broad
    Best for Sensor networks Phone-tethered IoT

    Rule of thumb: ZigBee when the network has many devices and needs mesh coverage; BLE when the device talks to a phone or the network is small.

    6.4 WiFi — wireless local area networks¶

    6.4.1 The 802.11 family¶

    WiFi (IEEE 802.11) dominates local wireless — home, office, public hotspot. Every WiFi generation has improved peak rate, spectral efficiency, and features:

    Standard Year Brand Band Channel width Peak rate Key feature
    802.11 1997 — 2.4 GHz 20 MHz 2 Mbps Original
    802.11b 1999 — 2.4 GHz 20 MHz 11 Mbps DSSS, more range
    802.11a 1999 — 5 GHz 20 MHz 54 Mbps OFDM
    802.11g 2003 — 2.4 GHz 20 MHz 54 Mbps OFDM at 2.4 GHz
    802.11n 2009 WiFi 4 2.4/5 GHz 40 MHz 600 Mbps MIMO, channel bonding
    802.11ac 2013 WiFi 5 5 GHz 80/160 MHz 6.9 Gbps MU-MIMO DL, 256-QAM
    802.11ax 2019 WiFi 6 2.4/5 GHz 160 MHz 9.6 Gbps OFDMA, MU-MIMO UL/DL, 1024-QAM, TWT
    802.11be 2024 WiFi 7 2.4/5/6 GHz 320 MHz 46 Gbps 4096-QAM, Multi-Link (MLO), 16×16 MIMO
    802.11ay 2024 — 60 GHz 2.16 GHz 100 Gbps mmWave, for VR/AR

    The naming confusion. IEEE uses numbers (802.11ac); the WiFi Alliance introduced simpler names (WiFi 4, 5, 6, 7) starting in 2018 to avoid consumer confusion. Both are used; the numbered names are unambiguous for generations, so this course uses them.

    6.4.2 Key PHY technologies¶

    OFDM and its evolution:

    • WiFi 4 (802.11n) — OFDM, 20/40 MHz channels, MIMO.
    • WiFi 5 (802.11ac) — OFDM, 80/160 MHz, MU-MIMO downlink, 256-QAM.
    • WiFi 6 (802.11ax) — OFDMA, MU-MIMO uplink + downlink, 1024-QAM, longer OFDM symbol (4× — reduces guard band overhead).
    • WiFi 7 (802.11be) — 4096-QAM, 320 MHz channels, Multi-Link Operation (MLO — simultaneous transmission on multiple bands), 16×16 MIMO.

    OFDMA in WiFi 6 — the biggest change since WiFi 4. Instead of scheduling one user per channel (all subcarriers), the AP allocates resource units (RUs) to multiple users simultaneously. This is exactly what LTE/5G do, and it massively improves efficiency in crowded environments (stadiums, airports, offices).

    MIMO scaling:

    Generation MIMO
    WiFi 4 4×4
    WiFi 5 8×8 (with MU-MIMO DL)
    WiFi 6 8×8 (with MU-MIMO DL+UL)
    WiFi 7 16×16, plus Multi-Link

    Worked example 6.3 — WiFi 6 capacity in a crowded office¶

    Scenario: Office with 200 users, each with a phone and laptop (400 devices). AP is WiFi 6, 5 GHz, 80 MHz channel, 4×4 MIMO.

    Per-AP theoretical peak: ~2.4 Gbps (WiFi 6, 80 MHz, 4×4).

    Realistic per-AP throughput: 40–60% of peak in dense environment → ~1.2 Gbps.

    Per-user throughput (ideal 400 evenly split): 1.2 Gbps / 400 = 3 Mbps per device. Too slow for video calls.

    With 8 APs deployed (typical office): 8 × 1.2 Gbps = 9.6 Gbps total → 24 Mbps per device — acceptable for most uses.

    OFDMA advantage: WiFi 5 would give ~1 Gbps per AP but with one user per transmission (MU-MIMO limited to 4 DL streams). WiFi 6 OFDMA allows ~30 RUs per 80 MHz channel → up to 30 simultaneous users per transmission → much better at high density.

    The lesson: WiFi 6's OFDMA is about capacity in dense environments, not peak rate per user. Its biggest benefit is that a stadium or airport full of people doesn't collapse the network.

    6.4.3 WiFi bands and coexistence¶

    Band Channels Interference
    2.4 GHz 14 channels (varies by country), 20 MHz Very congested (Bluetooth, ZigBee, microwave)
    5 GHz 25+ channels (varies), 20–160 MHz Congested but more channels
    6 GHz (WiFi 6E) 59 channels of 20 MHz, or 14 of 160 MHz Empty — reserved for WiFi

    The 6 GHz band (5.925–7.125 GHz) was allocated for WiFi 6E and WiFi 7 in 2020–2021. No legacy devices — every WiFi 6E device supports 6 GHz. Clean spectrum means better performance, especially in dense environments.

    6.4.4 WiFi security¶

    Standard Year Cipher Status
    WEP 1997 RC4 Broken (crackable in minutes)
    WPA 2003 TKIP Deprecated
    WPA2 2004 AES-CCMP Good, still widely used
    WPA3 2018 AES-GCMP, SAE Current standard

    WPA3 improvements:

    • SAE (Simultaneous Authentication of Equals) — replaces the WPA2 PSK handshake, resistant to offline dictionary attacks.
    • Forward secrecy — captured traffic can't be decrypted even if the password is later compromised.
    • 192-bit security mode for enterprise/WPA3-Enterprise.

    Rule: never deploy WEP or WPA. WPA2 is the minimum for compatibility; WPA3 is recommended for new deployments.

    6.5 Ultra-Wideband (UWB)¶

    6.5.1 What is UWB?¶

    Ultra-Wideband (UWB) is a radio technology that transmits very short impulses (< 1 ns) across a very wide bandwidth (> 500 MHz), at very low power spectral density.

    FCC definition: any signal with fractional bandwidth > 20% or absolute bandwidth > 500 MHz, transmitting at power below −41.3 dBm/MHz.

    Key properties:

    Property Value
    Frequency 3.1–10.6 GHz
    Bandwidth > 500 MHz
    Pulse duration 0.1–1 ns
    Range 1–10 m (typical), up to 30 m
    Data rate 100 Mbps–1 Gbps (short range)
    Power 0.5 mW (very low)
    Position accuracy ~10 cm

    6.5.2 Why UWB is different¶

    Traditional radio (narrowband): transmission occupies a narrow slice of spectrum around a carrier; the signal must be modulated onto that carrier (AM, FM, PSK, QAM).

    UWB: transmits impulses — extremely short pulses that inherently span a wide bandwidth. No carrier in the traditional sense; the "carrier" is the pulse repetition rate.

    Fourier transform intuition: a pulse of duration \(\tau\) has bandwidth \(\approx 1/\tau\). A 1 ns pulse = 1 GHz bandwidth. UWB exploits this directly.

    The advantage: wide bandwidth → fine time resolution → excellent ranging (position estimation). UWB can measure distances to cm precision by measuring time of flight.

    6.5.3 Two flavours of UWB¶

    1. Impulse Radio UWB (IR-UWB) — the original approach.

    • Transmit Gaussian pulses or monocycle pulses.
    • Data rate limited by pulse repetition frequency (typically 100–1000 Mbps).
    • Used in research and short-range radar.

    2. Multi-Band OFDM (MB-OFDM) — a competitor (WiMedia Alliance).

    • Divide the 3.1–10.6 GHz band into 14 sub-bands of 528 MHz.
    • Use OFDM within each sub-band.
    • Higher data rate (~1 Gbps), more complex.
    • WiMedia dissolved around 2010; MB-OFDM is now rarely used.

    3. IEEE 802.15.4z (the current standard) — supports both.

    • HRP (High Rate Pulse) UWB — for high data rate.
    • LRP (Low Rate Pulse) UWB — for low-power, precise ranging.
    • Focus on secure ranging (STS — Scrambled Timestamp Sequence) to prevent relay attacks.

    6.5.4 UWB applications¶

    High-precision indoor positioning.

    • Apple AirTag, Samsung SmartTag+ — UWB provides cm-level direction and distance, much better than Bluetooth RSSI.
    • Smart home — precise location of people and objects.
    • Warehouse logistics — track items to cm.

    Secure car access.

    • Digital car keys use UWB ranging to verify the key is actually nearby (defeats relay attacks that fool Bluetooth/RSSI systems).
    • Standardized by the Car Connectivity Consortium (CCC Digital Key 3.0).

    Industrial positioning.

    • Robots, tools, people in factories, mines, warehouses.
    • 10 cm precision, better than Bluetooth or WiFi for industrial use.

    Short-range high-rate data.

    • Wireless USB (defunct).
    • VR/AR cable replacement (in some products).
    • Wireless HDMI (few consumer products).

    Worked example 6.4 — UWB ranging precision¶

    UWB range resolution: governed by the pulse bandwidth \(B\):

    \[ \boxed{\;\Delta d \approx \frac{c}{2B}\;} \]

    For \(B = 1\) GHz: \(\Delta d \approx 3 \times 10^8 / (2 \times 10^9) = \mathbf{15\ cm}\).

    For \(B = 500\) MHz: \(\Delta d \approx \mathbf{30\ cm}\).

    Time-of-flight measurement: at \(d = 10\) m, the round-trip time is:

    \[ t = \frac{2d}{c} = \frac{20}{3 \times 10^8} \approx 67\ \text{ns}. \]

    A 1 ns timing error gives \(c \cdot t/2 = 15\) cm distance error — hence nanosecond-class timing is required for cm-level ranging. Modern UWB chips achieve < 100 ps timing precision.

    The lesson: UWB's ranging accuracy comes from the short pulse (wide bandwidth). No narrowband technology can match this.

    6.6 LPWAN — Low-Power Wide Area Networks¶

    Between WiFi (short-range, high-rate) and cellular (long-range, high-rate, power-hungry) there's a gap: long-range, very low power, low data rate, battery- powered. That's LPWAN — the IoT connectivity solution.

    6.6.1 The LPWAN landscape¶

    Technology Frequency Data rate Range Battery life Standard
    LoRa Sub-GHz ISM (868/915 MHz) 0.3–37.5 kbps 2–15 km 10+ years LoRa Alliance (proprietary PHY)
    NB-IoT Licensed LTE (700/800/900 MHz) 26–127 kbps 1–10 km 5–10 years 3GPP (Release 13)
    Sigfox Sub-GHz ISM (868/902 MHz) 100 bps 3–50 km 10+ years Sigfox (proprietary)
    LTE-M Licensed LTE 1 Mbps 1–10 km 5–10 years 3GPP (eMTC)
    Weightless Sub-GHz ISM 0.1–100 kbps 2–5 km 5+ years Weightless SIG

    Rule of thumb: LoRa for unlicensed global deployment, NB-IoT for licensed spectrum (tied to cellular operators), Sigfox for ultra-low data rate, LTE-M for higher data rate than NB-IoT.

    6.6.2 LoRa — the PHY and MAC¶

    LoRa = Long Range. The physical layer is proprietary (Semtech); the MAC layer (LoRaWAN) is open and managed by the LoRa Alliance.

    Chirp Spread Spectrum (CSS) — the modulation technique.

    • Each symbol is a frequency chirp (up-sweep or down-sweep).
    • Spreading factor (SF) controls chirp rate: SF7–SF12.
    • Higher SF = longer range (more spreading) but lower data rate.
    • SF12 gives ~-137 dBm sensitivity (very long range), at 250 bps.
    • SF7 gives ~-123 dBm, at ~5.5 kbps.

    The trade-off:

    SF Data rate Sensitivity Range (km)
    7 5.5 kbps -123 dBm 2
    9 1.8 kbps -129 dBm 6
    11 0.5 kbps -134 dBm 10
    12 0.25 kbps -137 dBm 15

    LoRaWAN classes:

    • Class A (baseline) — bidirectional; uplink scheduled by the device, two short downlink windows after each uplink. Lowest power.
    • Class B — beacon-synchronized; scheduled downlink receive windows. Moderate power, low downlink latency.
    • Class C — continuous receive (except when transmitting). Highest power, lowest latency. For mains-powered devices.

    Network architecture:

    • End devices — sensors that transmit.
    • Gateways — receive on multiple channels/SFs; forward to network server via IP (WiFi, Ethernet, cellular).
    • Network server — de-duplicates, manages devices, routing.
    • Application server — user applications.

    The key advantage: LoRa is license-free (ISM bands) — anyone can deploy a LoRaWAN network. Enterprises deploy private LoRa for building automation, agriculture, industrial sensors. National LoRaWAN networks exist in many countries (mobile operators, IoT providers).

    6.6.3 NB-IoT — cellular for IoT¶

    NB-IoT (Narrowband IoT) is the 3GPP IoT standard (Release 13, 2016).

    Key parameters:

    Parameter Value
    Bandwidth 180 kHz (single LTE resource block)
    Frequency Licensed LTE bands (in-band, guard-band, or standalone)
    Data rate 26 kbps UL, 127 kbps DL (multi-tone)
    Latency 1.6–10 s (longer than LTE)
    Battery life ~10 years (with PSM/eDRX)
    Range ~10–15 km (with repetitions, +20 dB over LTE)

    Key features:

    • Coverage extension: up to 20 dB (100×) over LTE via repetitions — the same message sent 2048 times, combined at the base station. Range up to ~15 km.
    • Power Saving Mode (PSM): the device sleeps for up to 413 days, waking only to send data. This is the key to 10-year battery life.
    • eDRX (extended Discontinuous Reception): periodic listening, up to 2.9 hours between wake-ups.
    • Extended coverage but at the cost of latency — messages take seconds to be delivered.

    Advantages over LoRa:

    • Licensed spectrum — no interference, guaranteed QoS.
    • Integrated with cellular — the operator handles the network, no gateway needed.
    • Higher data rate than LoRa (~100 kbps vs. ~5 kbps).
    • Global roaming via the operator's network.

    Disadvantages:

    • Needs cellular coverage — rural areas without LTE coverage can't use NB-IoT.
    • Operator-controlled — you pay per device, per month.
    • Higher device cost than LoRa.

    Worked example 6.5 — IoT battery life for LoRa vs. NB-IoT¶

    Scenario: A soil moisture sensor transmits 20 bytes every 30 minutes.

    LoRa (SF12, 250 bps):

    • Transmit time: 20 bytes × 8 bits / 250 bps = 0.64 s per transmission.
    • Transmit current (typical): 40 mA.
    • Per transmission: 0.64 s × 40 mA = 25.6 mA·s = 0.0071 mAh.
    • Per day (48 transmissions): 48 × 0.0071 = 0.341 mAh/day.
    • Sleep current: 1 μA × 24 h = 0.024 mAh/day.
    • Daily total: ~0.365 mAh.
    • Battery (2000 mAh): ~5,480 days = ~15 years (theoretical).

    NB-IoT:

    • Transmit time: 20 bytes × 8 bits / 26 kbps = 0.006 s per transmission (but with repetitions for coverage, effectively ~1 s).
    • Transmit current: 200 mA.
    • Per transmission: 1 s × 200 mA = 0.056 mAh.
    • Per day: 48 × 0.056 = 2.7 mAh/day.
    • Sleep (PSM): 5 μA × 24 h = 0.12 mAh/day.
    • Daily total: ~2.8 mAh/day.
    • Battery (5000 mAh, typical for NB-IoT devices): ~1,800 days = ~5 years (theoretical).

    Realistic: LoRa ~7–10 years, NB-IoT ~3–5 years — battery life is limited by sleep current and self-discharge, not by transmissions.

    The takeaway: LoRa is more power-efficient for very low-rate, sparse data. NB-IoT is worth the higher power for higher data rate and licensed spectrum.

    6.7 5G and IoT — the converged vision¶

    6.7.1 5G's three use cases¶

    The ITU defined IMT-2020 (5G) around three broad use cases:

    1. eMBB — enhanced Mobile Broadband.

    • Peak rate: 20 Gbps.
    • User-experienced rate: 100 Mbps–1 Gbps.
    • Use: 4K/8K video, AR/VR, cloud gaming.
    • Characteristics: high throughput, moderate latency (~4 ms).

    2. URLLC — Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication.

    • Latency: 1 ms (air interface).
    • Reliability: 99.999% (five nines).
    • Use: industrial automation, remote surgery, autonomous driving, smart grid.
    • Characteristics: latency-critical, high reliability, moderate data rate.

    3. mMTC — massive Machine-Type Communication.

    • Density: 1 million devices per km².
    • Battery: 10+ years.
    • Use: IoT sensors, smart city, agriculture, asset tracking.
    • Characteristics: high density, low data rate, low power.

    The 5G triangle: the three use cases pull in different directions. eMBB wants high rate; URLLC wants low latency; mMTC wants high density and low power. 5G NR is designed to serve all three, often on the same network via network slicing.

    6.7.2 5G NR — the new radio¶

    5G NR (New Radio) is the 3GPP air interface introduced in Release 15 (2018). It's a new PHY, not an evolution of LTE.

    Key PHY parameters:

    Aspect LTE 5G NR
    Frequency < 6 GHz < 6 GHz (FR1) + mmWave 24–52 GHz (FR2)
    Subcarrier spacing 15 kHz fixed 15, 30, 60, 120, 240 kHz (scalable)
    Channel bandwidth up to 20 MHz up to 100 MHz (FR1), 400 MHz (FR2)
    MIMO up to 8×8 up to 64×64 (FR1), 256 antennas (FR2 massive MIMO)
    Modulation up to 256-QAM up to 1024-QAM
    Latency (user plane) ~10 ms ~1 ms
    Peak DL 3 Gbps (LTE-A Pro) 20 Gbps
    Spectral efficiency ~6 bps/Hz (LTE-A) ~30 bps/Hz (5G)

    Scalable numerology — the key 5G innovation. Unlike LTE's fixed 15 kHz subcarrier spacing, 5G uses different subcarrier spacings for different scenarios:

    • 15 kHz — for wide-area coverage (like LTE).
    • 30 kHz — for urban (typical 5G).
    • 60 kHz — for low-latency (URLLC).
    • 120/240 kHz — for mmWave (high throughput).

    Short subcarriers → longer symbols → higher throughput. Longer subcarriers → shorter symbols → lower latency. Numerologies are chosen per application.

    Massive MIMO. 5G deploys 64–256 antenna elements at the base station:

    • Beamforming gain: ~18 dB (64 elements) to 24 dB (256 elements).
    • Multi-user MIMO: up to 16 UEs simultaneously.
    • 3D beamforming: both horizontal and vertical steering.

    mmWave. FR2 (24–52 GHz) provides 400 MHz channels — 8× wider than sub-6 GHz. This is what enables 20 Gbps peak rate. But mmWave has:

    • High path loss (Chapter 1 worked example 1.1: ~30 dB more than 2 GHz).
    • Blockage (rain, hands, walls).
    • Short range (100–300 m per cell).
    • Requires dense small-cell deployment.

    6.7.3 5G Core (5GC) — service-based architecture¶

    The 5G core network is a fundamental redesign from LTE's EPC. Instead of network elements connected by point-to-point interfaces, 5GC uses network functions (NFs) connected by service-based interfaces (SBI) — essentially a microservices architecture.

    Key NFs:

    Function Role
    AMF (Access & Mobility) Registration, mobility, connection management (control plane)
    SMF (Session Management) PDU session setup, IP address, QoS (control plane)
    UPF (User Plane Function) Packet routing, QoS enforcement, anchor point
    AUSF (Authentication) 5G authentication
    UDM (Unified Data Mgmt) Subscriber data, authentication vectors (like HSS)
    PCF (Policy Control) QoS policy, charging rules
    NSSF (Network Slicing Selection) Selects the right network slice
    NRF (NF Repository) Service discovery (NFs find each other)
    NEF (Network Exposure) APIs for third parties to use 5G capabilities
    AF (Application Function) Application server (owns the QoS request)

    Service-based interface (SBI):

    • All NFs communicate via HTTP/2 + JSON on the SBI.
    • Any NF can expose services that others consume.
    • NRF provides service discovery (like DNS for NFs).

    The advantage: flexible, cloud-native — 5GC can run on containers in a data center, orchestrated by Kubernetes, updated without touching the radio.

    6.7.4 Network slicing¶

    Network slicing is 5G's flagship feature — the ability to create virtual, isolated networks on the same physical infrastructure, each with its own resources, QoS, and topology.

    Each slice has:

    • Its own QoS and KPIs (latency, throughput, reliability).
    • Its own network functions (or shared, isolated by slice).
    • Its own resource allocation (spectrum, compute, backhaul).
    • Its own management and orchestration.

    Example slices:

    • eMBB slice — high throughput, moderate latency, for consumer broadband.
    • URLLC slice — 1 ms latency, 99.999% reliability, for industrial control.
    • mMTC slice — massive device count, low power, for IoT sensors.
    • V2X slice — vehicle-to-everything, low latency, high reliability.

    The NSSF (Network Slice Selection Function) selects the right slice for each UE based on subscription, requested service, and network policy.

    Why slicing matters: before 5G, an operator had one network serving all traffic. An industrial customer wanting guaranteed 1 ms latency competed with consumers streaming video. Slicing lets the operator sell dedicated capacity to an enterprise with strict QoS, without degrading the consumer network — a new business model.

    6.7.5 Edge computing and 5G¶

    Edge computing — moving compute resources close to the user, at cell towers or local data centers, instead of centralized cloud.

    Why 5G needs it:

    • URLLC latency: 1 ms air-interface latency is useless if the application server is 1,000 km away (adds 20–40 ms round-trip). Multi-access edge computing (MEC) puts the server at the base station or local edge.

    Use cases:

    • AR/VR — sub-20 ms end-to-end, must be local.
    • Industrial automation — real-time control loops, local.
    • Autonomous vehicles — safety-critical, local.
    • Cloud gaming — low-latency, local rendering.

    5G + MEC = network + compute at the edge = platform for real-time applications.

    6.7.6 IoT connectivity in 5G¶

    mMTC in 5G is delivered by three paths:

    1. NB-IoT and LTE-M — the same LPWAN standards, integrated into the 5G core (they become 5G-connected devices via dual-mode radios).
    2. 5G NR RedCap (Reduced Capability) — a lighter 5G NR variant for IoT devices that need more than NB-IoT but not full 5G (wearables, cameras, sensors with moderate data rate).
    3. 5G NR for IoT — for the rare IoT use case that needs URLLC or eMBB.

    Network slicing lets operators create a dedicated mMTC slice with massive device density and low power, without impacting eMBB users.

    The 5G IoT ecosystem combines:

    • Massive MIMO + beamforming for wide coverage.
    • mmWave small cells for dense IoT deployment.
    • Edge computing for real-time processing.
    • NB-IoT / LTE-M / RedCap for device-side connectivity.
    • Network slicing for isolation and QoS.

    Worked example 6.6 — network slicing for smart factory¶

    Scenario: A smart factory needs:

    • 500 industrial sensors sending 100 bytes every 1 s. mMTC slice.
    • 20 AGVs (automated guided vehicles) with 1 ms latency control loops. URLLC slice.
    • 10 HD cameras streaming 4K video for quality inspection. eMBB slice.
    • AR glasses for technicians, remote expert assistance. eMBB + URLLC mix.

    Without network slicing: one shared network. Under congestion, AGV control latency spikes → safety risk.

    With slicing:

    • mMTC slice: 500 sensors × 100 bytes/s = 50 kbps. Trivial. Reserve 1 MHz.
    • URLLC slice: 20 AGVs × 100 kbps with 1 ms latency. Reserve dedicated RBs + UPF at factory edge. 5 MHz.
    • eMBB slice: 10 cameras × 20 Mbps = 200 Mbps. 20 MHz + MU-MIMO.
    • AR/VR slice: mix of eMBB + URLLC. 10 MHz.

    Total: 36 MHz reserved, each slice isolated. AGV control latency guaranteed < 1 ms even during video peak traffic. Business model: factory pays for QoS guaranteed slices.

    6.8 Choosing the right connectivity¶

    For any given IoT application, choosing between WPAN / WLAN / LPWAN / cellular depends on four variables:

    Parameter WPAN WLAN LPWAN Cellular
    Range 10–100 m 30–100 m 2–15 km 1–30 km
    Data rate 0.1–3 Mbps 100 Mbps–30 Gbps 0.1–127 kbps 100 Mbps–20 Gbps
    Power Very low High Very low High (except NB-IoT)
    Cost Low Moderate Low High (spectrum)

    Decision flowchart:

    Start
  │
  ├─ Data rate > 1 Mbps?
  │   ├─ Yes → Local → WiFi
  │   │         → Mobile → 4G/5G
  │   └─ No → continue
  │
  ├─ Range > 100 m?
  │   ├─ Yes → Licensed spectrum?
  │   │         ├─ Yes → NB-IoT / LTE-M
  │   │         └─ No  → LoRa / Sigfox
  │   └─ No → continue
  │
  ├─ Battery-powered & low duty cycle?
  │   ├─ Yes → BLE / ZigBee / LoRa
  │   └─ No  → WiFi / Bluetooth Classic
  │
  └─ Latency-critical?
      ├─ Yes → Cellular (4G/5G)
      └─ No  → LPWAN

    Worked example 6.7 — choosing connectivity¶

    Application: An agricultural sensor measuring soil moisture, sending 50 bytes every hour, powered by a small solar panel with battery backup.

    Requirements: - Data rate: 50 bytes/hour = 0.11 bps. Very low. - Range: field is 1 km wide; nearest road/power 5 km. Long range. - Power: solar/battery — should last years. Very low power. - Cost: many devices (100 sensors), so low per-device cost.

    Options:

    Option Fit
    WiFi ❌ Range too short (100 m)
    Bluetooth ❌ Range too short (100 m)
    ZigBee ❌ Range too short (100 m), no backhaul
    LoRa ✅ Range 10+ km, low power, low cost
    NB-IoT ✅ Range 10 km (if coverage), low power, needs cellular subscription
    Sigfox ✅ Range 30+ km, ultra-low power, ultra-low data rate

    Best fit: LoRaWAN — deploy a private LoRaWAN gateway at the farmhouse (powered by solar), sensors use LoRa. No monthly cellular fees, cm-level position (optional), 5+ year battery life on AA cells. If cellular coverage is available and reliable, NB-IoT also works but with monthly subscription cost.

    Why not 5G mMTC? Overkill — the data rate, latency, and density are far below what 5G offers; the cost is 10× higher.

    6.9 Chapter summary¶

    1. NGN = packet-based, all-IP, multi-service network. It evolved from voice-over-IP convergence (2000s) to fixed-mobile convergence (2010s) to IoT convergence (2020s).
    2. Bluetooth Classic — for continuous data (audio, files). Piconet topology, frequency hopping, 2–3 Mbps. BLE — for bursty sensor data; 40 channels, 3 ms connection, < 1 μA sleep, up to 400 m range (BT 5.0).
    3. ZigBee (IEEE 802.15.4) — mesh network for sensor networks. 250 kbps, AODV routing, 65,000+ nodes, < 1 μA sleep. Dominant in home and industrial automation.
    4. WiFi (802.11) — the local wireless standard. WiFi 6 (802.11ax) brings OFDMA and MU-MIMO uplink for high-density environments. WiFi 7 (802.11be) adds 320 MHz channels, 4096-QAM, and Multi-Link Operation. The 6 GHz band provides clean spectrum for WiFi 6E/7.
    5. UWB — impulse radio using very short pulses (sub-ns) across > 500 MHz bandwidth. Enables ~10 cm precision positioning, used in AirTags, car keys, warehouse tracking.
    6. LPWAN — low-power wide-area networks for IoT. LoRa (unlicensed, chirp spread spectrum, 2–15 km), NB-IoT (licensed, cellular, 10 km, 10-year battery), Sigfox (ultra-low rate, very long range).
    7. 5G NR — new PHY with scalable numerology (15–240 kHz), massive MIMO (64–256 antennas), mmWave bands, 1 ms latency, 20 Gbps peak. FR1 (Sub-6) and FR2 (mmWave) serve different scenarios.
    8. 5G Core (5GC) — service-based architecture with microservices (AMF, SMF, UPF, etc.) communicating via HTTP/2. Cloud-native, orchestrated by Kubernetes.
    9. Network slicing — virtual isolated networks on one physical infrastructure, each with its own QoS and topology. Enables URLLC/eMBB/mMTC coexistence and new business models.
    10. Edge computing (MEC) — brings compute close to the user, essential for URLLC. 5G + MEC is the platform for real-time applications.
    11. IoT connectivity — WPAN (BLE, ZigBee) for local, WLAN (WiFi) for local high-rate, LPWAN (LoRa, NB-IoT) for wide-area low-power, cellular (4G/5G) for high-rate with QoS. Choose based on range, rate, power, and cost.

    C. Define / explain — model answers
    1. NGN: a packet-based, all-IP network providing telecommunication services (voice, data, video) with QoS, decoupled from the underlying transport. Three stages: (i) all-IP convergence (2000s) — replacing PSTN with VoIP, circuit-switched cores with IP; (ii) fixed-mobile convergence (2010s) — unify fixed and mobile under one core, VoWiFi, LTE-WiFi; (iii) IoT convergence (2020s) — LPWAN, 5G mMTC, edge computing, network slicing.
    2. Bluetooth Classic: continuous data, piconet topology, 2–3 Mbps, higher power. BLE: bursty sensor data, 40 channels of 2 MHz, 3 ms connection, < 1 μA sleep, up to 400 m. Use: Classic for headphones, audio streaming, file transfer; BLE for wearables, fitness trackers, battery-powered sensors.
    3. ZigBee mesh: any router can forward traffic for any other. AODV: on-demand routing — source broadcasts Route Request (RREQ), destination replies with Route Reply (RREP), routers cache routes for future use. Why mesh: extends range beyond individual node range (multi-hop), improves reliability (redundant paths), and handles node failures gracefully. For sensor networks that can't afford fiber backhaul to every node, mesh is essential.
    4. WiFi 5 (802.11ac): 5 GHz, 80/160 MHz, MU-MIMO DL, 256-QAM. WiFi 6 (802.11ax): adds OFDMA, MU-MIMO UL, 1024-QAM, TWT (Target Wake Time for IoT power), 4× longer OFDM symbol. Better at high density. WiFi 7 (802.11be): 320 MHz channels, 4096-QAM, Multi-Link Operation (MLO — simultaneous transmission on 2.4, 5, 6 GHz), 16×16 MIMO.
    5. UWB: transmits very short pulses (sub-ns) which inherently span > 500 MHz bandwidth. High precision: Fourier transform — pulse of duration τ has bandwidth ≈ 1/τ. Wide bandwidth gives fine time resolution (τ ~ ns), and time of flight directly gives distance: d = c·t/2. So cm-precision ranging is possible, unlike narrowband RSSI (which has ~1 m precision).
    6. LoRa: unlicensed ISM, Chirp Spread Spectrum, 2–15 km, 0.3–37.5 kbps, private or national network. Best for enterprise IoT, agriculture, building automation, remote sensing. NB-IoT: licensed LTE, 180 kHz bandwidth, 1–10 km, ~100 kbps, uses PSM for 10-year battery. Best for operator-managed IoT (smart meters, tracking). Sigfox: proprietary, ultra-low data rate (100 bps), very long range (up to 50 km), ultra-low power. Best for asset tracking, simple telemetry with very infrequent updates.
    7. LoRa SF trade-off: SF7–SF12 correspond to 2^7 to 2^12 chips per symbol. Higher SF: longer symbol time → more signal energy per bit → better sensitivity (−137 dBm for SF12) → longer range (up to 15 km). But: lower data rate (250 bps for SF12 vs. 5.5 kbps for SF7) → longer transmission time → higher energy per transmission. Trade-off: range vs. data rate vs. battery life.
    8. eMBB: enhanced Mobile Broadband — high rate (100 Mbps–1 Gbps user), for 4K/8K video, AR/VR, cloud gaming. URLLC: Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication — 1 ms latency, 99.999% reliability, for industrial automation, remote surgery, autonomous driving. mMTC: massive Machine-Type Communication — 1M devices/km², low power, for IoT sensors, smart city, agriculture.
    9. 5G scalable numerology: LTE uses fixed 15 kHz subcarrier spacing. 5G NR supports 15, 30, 60, 120, 240 kHz. Why: different scenarios need different trade-offs. Short subcarriers (15 kHz) → long symbol duration → robust for high Doppler / wide-area. Long subcarriers (120/240 kHz) → short symbol duration → low latency (for URLLC) but need higher SNR. Deployments choose numerology per scenario — 15 kHz for macro coverage, 30 kHz for urban 5G, 60/120 kHz for mmWave.
    10. Network slicing: ability to create multiple virtual isolated networks on the same physical infrastructure, each with its own QoS, topology, resources, and management. New business models: operator can sell dedicated URLLC slices to industrial customers with guaranteed latency, eMBB slices to consumers, mMTC slices for IoT — each priced for its SLA. Before slicing, all traffic shared one network; the industrial customer's 1 ms latency was at the mercy of consumers streaming video. Slicing provides QoS isolation as a product.
    11. MEC (Multi-access Edge Computing): compute resources placed at cell towers / local data centers, close to the user. Complements 5G: 5G's 1 ms air-interface latency is useless if the application server is 1000 km away (adds 20+ ms). MEC brings the server to the edge → full end-to-end latency in 5–10 ms. Use cases: AR/VR (sub-20 ms), industrial control loops (1 ms), autonomous vehicles (safety-critical), cloud gaming (low-latency rendering), real-time video analytics.
    12. Smart city, 100,000 sensors over 50 km²: 2,000 devices/km². LoRa or NB-IoT are the only viable options. WiFi covers 100 m per AP, would need 5,000 APs and power for each — infeasible. Cellular works but 100,000 subscriptions are expensive. LoRaWAN: 20 gateways cover 50 km² (2.5 km radius each with SF12), private network, low per-device cost, 10-year battery. NB-IoT: if cellular coverage exists and 5–10 year battery acceptable, operator-managed is simpler. Recommendation: LoRa for maximum flexibility, NB-IoT for integration with city's cellular infrastructure.

    Suggested tutorial problems¶

    1. BLE battery life. A BLE beacon transmits every 1 s at 10 mA for 1 ms. Sleep current 5 μA. Battery 250 mAh (CR2032). Compute battery life. Then recompute if advertising interval is 10 s. What's the trade-off?

    2. ZigBee routing. Design a ZigBee mesh for a 200 m × 200 m factory. Nodes every 20 m, coverage 25 m. How many routers? How many hops from corner to corner? What's the approximate latency?

    3. WiFi deployment. An airport terminal has 10,000 m², 5000 concurrent users. Design a WiFi 6E deployment. How many APs? What channel plan? What's per-user peak rate?

    4. UWB precision. UWB with 1.5 GHz bandwidth. Compute range resolution. If timing error is 200 ps, what's the distance error? Is it enough for AirTag-style object finding?

    5. LoRa trade-off. Sensor transmits 10 bytes. Compute airtime for SF7, SF10, SF12. Which SF maximizes battery life for a 1 km deployment?

    6. NB-IoT coverage. NB-IoT with 20 dB coverage extension vs. LTE. LTE covers 2 km per cell. What's the NB-IoT range with the same path-loss model? Is this realistic?

    7. 5G peak rate. 5G NR in mmWave, 400 MHz bandwidth, 64-QAM, 8-layer MIMO, rate-3/4 coding. Compute peak rate. Then compute with 256-QAM (5G NR's maximum).

    8. Network slicing. A hospital needs: 1000 IoT sensors, 50 video streams (20 Mbps each), 10 robotic surgery links (1 ms latency). Design three slices. Estimate resources for each.

    9. Edge computing latency. An AR application needs 10 ms end-to-end. UE air interface = 1 ms, edge compute = 2 ms, application = 3 ms. Can you meet the budget? What if you use centralized cloud (20 ms network RTT)?

    10. Connectivity choice. Compare LoRa, NB-IoT, and 5G RedCap for: (a) water meter reading, (b) warehouse asset tracking, (c) agricultural soil moisture, (d) vehicle telemetry. Justify each choice.

    11. WiFi 7 Multi-Link. A WiFi 7 AP supports 2.4 GHz (40 MHz), 5 GHz (160 MHz), 6 GHz (320 MHz) MLO. Compute total peak rate assuming 4×4 MIMO, 4096-QAM. Is this 46 Gbps?

    12. 5G vs. 4G for IoT. For each of the three 5G use cases (eMBB, URLLC, mMTC), compare the 5G requirements vs. LTE capability. For each, estimate how much better 5G is (factor of improvement).

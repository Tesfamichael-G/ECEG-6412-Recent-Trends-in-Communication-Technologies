    Wired Technology Trends (Part II)¶

    Chapter at a glance

    Contact hours: 3 lecture · Home study: 7 h · Part II — Wired technology trends

    Coverage (syllabus): wired technologies for access loop · core network · distribution networks.

    Learning outcomes¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

    1. Explain why wired infrastructure is the invisible foundation of every wireless network — and quantify how much of mobile traffic rides on fiber.
    2. Compare access-loop technologies — PON, xDSL, DOCSIS, Ethernet — in capacity, distance, and deployment cost.
    3. Compute PON capacity and split-ratio limits for GPON, XG-PON, XGS-PON, and NG-PON2.
    4. Describe core-network evolution — from TDM/SDH to DWDM + IP/MPLS — and compute DWDM capacity per fiber.
    5. Explain distribution networks — metro Ethernet, carrier Ethernet services, ring vs. mesh topologies — and the MEF service definitions.
    6. Trace mobile backhaul — why fiber is preferred, when microwave is used, and what the capacity and latency requirements are for 4G/5G.
    7. Compute CPRI/eCPRI bandwidth for C-RAN and describe timing requirements (SyncE, IEEE 1588 PTP).
    8. Analyse the cost per bit of wired vs. wireless access and explain why wired wins for fixed, wireless wins for mobile.

    1. The invisible foundation of wireless¶

    1.1 Every wireless call is mostly wired¶

    A 5G "wireless" connection is 10–1000× more wired than wireless by distance:

    UE ──[radio]── gNB ──[fiber]── aggregation ──[fiber]── core ──[fiber]── internet
     100 m       10 km          100 km           1000+ km

    The radio link is the last 100 m–1 km. Everything else is fiber or copper. This is true of:

    • 4G/5G mobile — the gNB's backhaul is fiber (or microwave for rural).
    • WiFi — the AP's uplink is Ethernet or fiber.
    • Fixed broadband — FTTH, xDSL, DOCSIS all terminate in fiber.

    The premise of this chapter: understanding wireless networks without understanding their wired infrastructure is like understanding a city's traffic by looking only at the parking lots. The wired network sets the capacity, latency, and cost envelope within which wireless operates.

    1.2 The three tiers of wired infrastructure¶

    Tier Function Typical technology Distance
    Access loop Connects individual subscribers (homes, businesses, cell sites) to the operator's first aggregation point PON, xDSL, DOCSIS, Ethernet 100 m – 20 km
    Distribution / metro Aggregates access traffic across a city into a few core points Carrier Ethernet, IP/MPLS, DWDM 5 – 100 km
    Core Transports traffic between cities, countries, continents DWDM, IP/MPLS, OTN 100 – 10,000 km

    Each tier has its own economics and technology, and each has evolved dramatically over the last 20 years.

    1.3 Why "wired" still matters in a wireless course¶

    Three reasons:

    1. Mobile backhaul is the bottleneck. A 5G gNB produces 10–20 Gbps peak traffic; the fiber backhaul must carry it. Backhaul often lags radio capacity by 3–5 years — the network can transmit faster than it can deliver.

    2. Fixed-mobile convergence (FMC). 5G and WiFi integrate deeply — traffic from a WiFi network may be handed to 5G core; voice calls over WiFi (VoWiFi) use the same IMS as VoLTE. The wired backhaul is what makes convergence possible.

    3. The economics of data. Wired transmission costs ~\(0.001/GB**; wireless costs **~\)1–10/GB. The 1000× difference explains why operators push traffic to WiFi and fiber wherever possible — the wireless radio is used only for the last hop.

    2. Access loop — connecting the subscriber¶

    2.1 The access loop problem¶

    The access loop (also called the last mile or local loop) connects each subscriber to the operator's first aggregation point. Historically this was copper twisted pair (telephone line), then coaxial cable (TV), and is increasingly fiber.

    The design constraints are severe:

    • Each subscriber needs a dedicated wire — the access loop is inherently point-to-point or point-to-multipoint.
    • Distance is limited by signal attenuation (copper: ~1 km for high rates, fiber: 20+ km).
    • Cost per subscriber is dominated by trenching/cabling — installing a new wire costs $500–2000 per home.

    The access loop is the most expensive part of the network in capex — often 40–60% of total deployment cost.

    2.2 Copper access — xDSL¶

    xDSL (Digital Subscriber Line) reuses the existing telephone twisted-pair copper for data, alongside voice (or replacing it).

    Technology evolution:

    Standard Year Downstream Upstream Distance
    ADSL 1999 8 Mbps 1 Mbps 3–5 km
    ADSL2+ 2003 24 Mbps 3.5 Mbps 1.5–3 km
    VDSL 2001 52 Mbps 16 Mbps 1–1.5 km
    VDSL2 2006 100 Mbps 100 Mbps 500 m – 1 km
    VDSL2 Vectoring 2010s 100–300 Mbps 100 Mbps up to 1 km
    G.fast 2014 500 Mbps–1 Gbps 500 Mbps 100–250 m
    G.mgfast 2018 up to 10 Gbps up to 10 Gbps < 100 m

    Key idea: xDSL trades distance for rate — the further from the exchange, the lower the achievable speed. Higher-rate variants use wider bandwidth, which attenuates more on copper — hence shorter reach.

    DSL from the curb/neighborhood. To extend high rates (VDSL, G.fast) to subscribers, operators deploy DSLAMs (DSL Access Multiplexers) close to homes — in cabinets, basements, or street-side — connected to the exchange by fiber (FTTC — fiber to the cabinet). The copper is then short (< 300 m), allowing high rates.

    Vectoring. Crosstalk between adjacent DSL lines limits rate. Vectoring coordinates multiple lines at the DSLAM to cancel crosstalk — recovers most of the rate that crosstalk would otherwise eat. It's the last big copper innovation before fiber.

    Why copper still matters: in some markets (especially rural and developing countries), the existing copper is too valuable to replace. xDSL operators squeeze 100–300 Mbps out of VDSL2 vectoring or G.fast — enough for most consumer needs — for a fraction of FTTH's capex.

    2.3 Coaxial cable — DOCSIS¶

    Cable TV networks use coaxial cable or HFC (Hybrid Fiber-Coax) — fiber to a neighborhood node, then coax to homes. Data is carried by DOCSIS (Data Over Cable Service Interface Specification).

    Standard Year Downstream Upstream Notes
    DOCSIS 1.0 1997 40 Mbps 10 Mbps First widespread cable modem
    DOCSIS 2.0 2001 40 Mbps 30 Mbps Improved upstream
    DOCSIS 3.0 2006 1 Gbps 200 Mbps Channel bonding (multiple 6 MHz channels)
    DOCSIS 3.1 2013 10 Gbps 1–2 Gbps OFDM, 4096-QAM, 192 MHz channels
    DOCSIS 4.0 2019 10 Gbps 6 Gbps Full duplex (simultaneous DL/UL on same spectrum)

    Channel bonding (DOCSIS 3.0+): aggregate multiple 6 MHz (or 8 MHz) channels into one logical "pipe" — 32+ channels downstream gives >1 Gbps.

    DOCSIS 3.1's OFDM: replaces single-carrier QAM with OFDM, allowing flexible channel widths, higher-order modulation (4096-QAM), and better spectral efficiency.

    HFC architecture:

    • CMTS (Cable Modem Termination System) at the headend — the DOCSIS endpoint.
    • Fiber node in each neighborhood — converts fiber to coax.
    • Coax from node to home — covers 500–2000 homes per node.

    Cable operators upgraded from all-coax (1990s) to HFC (2000s) — the fiber backbones allow huge data rates to each node, shared among 500–2000 homes.

    The sharing issue. Coax is a shared medium — all homes on a node compete for the same spectrum. In peak hours, this causes congestion. Fiber (PON) and xDSL are more dedicated (PON is shared at the splitter but has more headroom).

    2.4 Fiber access — PON¶

    PON (Passive Optical Network) is the dominant FTTH technology — passive splitters replace active electronics in the field, massively reducing cost and maintenance.

    PON architecture:

    OLT ──[fiber]── splitter 1:32 ──[fiber]── ONU (home)
    • OLT (Optical Line Terminal) at the operator's exchange.
    • Splitter — passive optical device (no power) that splits one fiber into 16–128 subscriber fibers.
    • ONU (Optical Network Unit) at the subscriber (or ONT, Optical Network Terminal, in the home).

    Downstream and upstream use different wavelengths (WDM): - Downstream: 1490 nm (typical). - Upstream: 1310 nm (typical). - A third wavelength (1550 nm) often carries video overlay.

    PON generations:

    Standard Year Downstream Upstream Split ratio Reach
    BPON 1998 155 Mbps 155 Mbps 1:32 20 km
    GPON 2003 2.5 Gbps 1.25 Gbps 1:128 20 km
    XG-PON 2010 10 Gbps 2.5 Gbps 1:128 20 km
    XGS-PON 2016 10 Gbps 10 Gbps 1:128 20 km
    NG-PON2 2015 40 Gbps 40 Gbps 1:64 40 km
    50G PON 2021+ 50 Gbps 25–50 Gbps 1:32 20 km

    Key insight: PON is shared — a 1:32 GPON splitter means 32 subscribers share 2.5 Gbps downstream. Per-user peak is ~80 Mbps, but if only 1 user is active, that user can get the full 2.5 Gbps. In practice, statistical multiplexing gives ~100 Mbps typical per user, enough for most residential needs.

    Worked example 2.1 — GPON capacity per user¶

    Scenario: A GPON with 1:32 split, 2.5 Gbps downstream, 32 subscribers. Busy-hour utilization: 60% of subscribers active concurrently, each demanding peak rate.

    Worst case (all 32 active simultaneously): Per-user rate = 2.5 Gbps / 32 = 78 Mbps.

    Realistic (60% active, statistical multiplexing): Per-user rate = 2.5 Gbps / 19 = 131 Mbps.

    What about XGS-PON? 10 Gbps / 32 = 312 Mbps per user (worst case); 10 Gbps / 19 = 526 Mbps (realistic).

    For a 4K video stream (~25 Mbps) + two HD streams (~5 Mbps each) + general web use (~10 Mbps): ~50 Mbps peak. GPON comfortably handles it, XGS-PON has ample headroom.

    2.5 Fiber to the Home — architectures¶

    Three deployment models:

    FTTH (Fiber to the Home) — fiber all the way to the subscriber's ONT. 2.5–10 Gbps per home.

    FTTB (Fiber to the Building) — fiber to a building, copper (VDSL, G.fast) or Ethernet to individual apartments. Cheaper for dense buildings, slightly lower per-apartment rates.

    FTTC / FTTdp (Fiber to the Cabinet/Distribution Point) — fiber to a street cabinet, copper (VDSL2, G.fast) for the last 100–500 m. Much cheaper than full FTTH, at the cost of copper-dependent rates (100–500 Mbps).

    Deployment economics:

    Architecture Capex per home Peak rate Time to deploy
    FTTC (VDSL2) $200–500 100 Mbps Fast (reuses copper)
    FTTB $500–1000 500 Mbps Moderate
    FTTH $1000–2500 1–10 Gbps Slow (needs trenching)

    Most operators deploy a mix — FTTH in new builds and dense urban, FTTC in older areas, upgrading to FTTH when copper needs replacing.

    2.6 Fixed wireless access (FWA) — the wired alternative¶

    FWA connects homes using wireless instead of fiber: - 5G FWA at 3.5 GHz or 28 GHz — 100 Mbps–1 Gbps per home, 1–5 km range. - mmWave FWA — up to 1 Gbps per home, 200–500 m range, requires LOS. - WiMAX (legacy) — mostly succeeded by 5G FWA.

    When FWA wins: - Rural areas where trenching fiber costs $2000+/home. - Rapid deployment — a base station serves 200 homes in weeks vs. months for fiber. - Temporary or mobile needs — construction sites, events.

    When fiber wins: - Dense urban — fiber is cheaper per home at high density, and has more capacity. - Long-term — fiber has 40+ year life, FWA needs periodic radio upgrades.

    The trend: operators deploy fiber where economics justify, FWA elsewhere — often 5G FWA for the last 20% of homes that fiber can't reach.

    3. Core network — the optical backbone¶

    3.1 The core network problem¶

    Core networks transport traffic between cities, countries, and continents. The fundamental constraint is: terabits per second over thousands of km, with sub-10-ms latency and 99.999% reliability.

    Two technologies dominate:

    • DWDM (Dense Wavelength Division Multiplexing) — optical transport.
    • IP/MPLS — packet routing.

    The modern core is a layered stack:

    Layer Technology Function
    L0 — Fiber Single-mode fiber Physical medium
    L1 — DWDM 80+ wavelengths × 100–400 Gbps Optical transport
    L2 — OTN Optical Transport Network Wavelength management, FEC
    L3 — IP/MPLS Label-switched packets Routing, traffic engineering

    3.2 DWDM — the capacity multiplier¶

    DWDM transmits multiple wavelengths on one fiber simultaneously — each wavelength carries an independent signal.

    Key parameters:

    • Wavelength spacing: 50 GHz or 100 GHz (ITU-T G.694.1 grid).
    • Band: C-band (1530–1565 nm, ~35 nm wide → ~80 channels at 50 GHz).
    • Per-wavelength rate: 100–400 Gbps (with advanced modulation and coherent detection).
    • Total per fiber: 80 × 100 Gbps = 8 Tbps, up to 80 × 400 Gbps = 32 Tbps.

    Newer bands:

    • L-band (1565–1625 nm) — doubles the spectrum; C+L DWDM reaches 64 Tbps.
    • Extended C+L (Super-C, Super-L) — pushes to 100+ Tbps.

    Coherent detection is the key enabling technology — coherent optical receivers recover both amplitude and phase, allowing higher-order modulation (16-QAM, 64-QAM on a single wavelength). Combined with DSP for electronic dispersion compensation and forward error correction, coherent DWDM achieves 100–400 Gbps per wavelength.

    Worked example 3.1 — DWDM capacity¶

    Scenario: A 100 GHz DWDM system on the C-band (1530–1565 nm, 35 nm ≈ 4.4 THz).

    Number of 100 GHz channels: 4.4 THz / 100 GHz ≈ 44 channels.

    Per-wavelength rate: 200 Gbps (coherent, 16-QAM).

    Total fiber capacity: 44 × 200 Gbps = 8.8 Tbps.

    With 400 Gbps per channel (coherent 64-QAM): 44 × 400 = 17.6 Tbps.

    With 50 GHz spacing (Super-C band): ~80 channels → 80 × 400 Gbps = 32 Tbps.

    Perspective: A single modern fiber pair carries the entire traffic of a mid-sized country. A transatlantic cable with 8 fiber pairs = 100–250 Tbps — enough for tens of millions of simultaneous 4K video streams.

    3.3 OTN — Optical Transport Network¶

    OTN (ITU-T G.709) provides: - Framing and multiplexing of client signals (Ethernet, SDH/SONET, Fibre Channel). - Forward error correction (FEC) — extends reach and reduces errors. - Operations, administration, maintenance (OAM) — monitoring, protection switching. - Tandem connection monitoring — end-to-end performance monitoring across operators.

    Common OTN rates:

    Container Rate Client
    OTU1 2.7 Gbps Gigabit Ethernet, STM-16
    OTU2 10.7 Gbps 10 Gigabit Ethernet
    OTU3 43 Gbps 40G Ethernet
    OTU4 111.8 Gbps 100G Ethernet
    OTUCn n × 100 Gbps FlexE, 400G

    The role of OTN is similar to SDH (its predecessor) — it "wraps" client traffic with error correction, monitoring, and protection. In a modern core, OTN handles 100G/400G wavelength management under IP/MPLS.

    3.4 IP/MPLS — the packet core¶

    IP/MPLS carries the actual traffic — it's the L3 layer that routers process.

    MPLS (Multiprotocol Label Switching) adds: - Label switching — fast forwarding based on labels instead of IP lookups. - Traffic engineering — explicit routes for specific traffic (avoiding congested links). - VPNs — L2VPN and L3VPN isolate customer traffic on shared infrastructure. - Fast reroute — sub-50 ms protection against link failures.

    How MPLS works:

    1. Ingress router classifies packets and adds a label (e.g., "traffic class gold, go via path A").
    2. Core routers forward based on the label — no IP lookup, no BGP table.
    3. Egress router removes the label and forwards the packet.

    Modern evolution — Segment Routing (SR):

    • SR-MPLS — labels encode a path as a list of segments (no per-flow state).
    • SRv6 — encodes paths as IPv6 addresses with segment lists. No MPLS needed.
    • Advantages: simpler operations, traffic engineering without RSVP-TE, better SDN compatibility.

    The trend: IP/MPLS is moving from "router-centric" to SDN-controlled — a centralized controller (e.g., Cisco Crosswork, Juniper Paragon, Nokia NSP) computes optimal paths and pushes segment lists to routers.

    3.5 SDN and NFV¶

    SDN (Software-Defined Networking) — decouple the control plane from the data plane.

    • Data plane: forwarding (hardware switches, routers).
    • Control plane: routing decisions (centralized controller).
    • Northbound API (to applications) — REST, gRPC.
    • Southbound API (to devices) — OpenFlow, NETCONF, gNMI, P4Runtime.

    Advantages: centralized visibility, programmability, rapid deployment of new services.

    NFV (Network Function Virtualization) — move network functions from dedicated hardware to virtual machines or containers running on commodity servers.

    • VNFs (Virtual Network Functions) — router, firewall, NAT, EPC, IMS as software.
    • MANO (Management and Orchestration) — deployment, scaling, lifecycle.

    Combined SDN + NFV: the network becomes a software platform — providers deploy new services in minutes, scale to handle traffic spikes, and update without hardware replacement.

    Where SDN/NFV are used:

    • 5G core — service-based architecture, cloud-native (see Chapter 6).
    • Data center networks — spine-leaf architecture, SDN controllers.
    • Wide-area networks (SD-WAN) — enterprise WAN over any transport.
    • Access networks — virtual OLTs, disaggregated RAN (O-RAN).

    3.6 Submarine cables — connecting continents¶

    99% of international data travels through submarine cables — not satellites. ~450 cables globally, ~1.4 million km total length.

    Modern submarine cables:

    • 8–24 fiber pairs per cable.
    • Each fiber pair carries 100+ Tbps (coherent DWDM).
    • Total per cable = ~1–20 Tbps (older) to ~100–250 Tbps (newer, e.g., Google's Dunant, Facebook's 2Africa).
    • Repeaters every 40–80 km — optical amplifiers that boost the signal.
    • Latency: ≈ 5 μs per km of fiber (speed of light in glass).

    The "latency race": for financial trading, a new cable that's 10% shorter is worth billions — because it shaves milliseconds off the London–New York round-trip. The Hibernia Express (2015) was built specifically to be the lowest-latency transatlantic route.

    Submarine cable economics:

    • Capex: $100M–500M per cable (multi-year project).
    • Capacity: 100+ Tbps per cable.
    • Cost per bit: <$0.0001/GB — the cheapest transport in the network.
    • Lifespan: 25 years (but capacity upgrades every 5 years as coherent optics improve).

    4. Distribution networks — metro and aggregation¶

    4.1 The distribution tier¶

    Between the access loop and the core, there's the distribution (or metro) network — aggregating traffic from many access nodes into a few core nodes.

    Typical structure:

    Access (thousands of homes) → Aggregation (10–100 nodes) → Core (2–10 nodes)

    Technologies:

    • Metro Ethernet — Ethernet-based aggregation, standard in urban areas.
    • IP/MPLS — for traffic that needs VPN, QoS, or traffic engineering.
    • DWDM — for very high capacity between metro nodes.
    • OTN — for transport-grade reliability.

    4.2 Carrier Ethernet¶

    Carrier Ethernet adapts Ethernet (designed for LANs) to operator requirements:

    • Reliability: 50 ms protection switching (like SDH).
    • Scalability: VLAN stacking (QinQ), 4K–16M service instances.
    • QoS: 8 traffic classes with strict priority.
    • OAM: IEEE 802.1ag (Connectivity Fault Management), Y.1731 (performance monitoring).

    MEF service definitions:

    Service Type Use case
    E-Line (EPL, EVPL) Point-to-point Business leased line, mobile backhaul
    E-LAN (EP-LAN, EVP-LAN) Multipoint (any-to-any) Enterprise VPN, metro Ethernet
    E-Tree (EP-Tree, EVP-Tree) Rooted multipoint Cable TV, video distribution
    • EPL/EP-LAN — dedicated (dedicated bandwidth per customer).
    • EVPL/EVP-LAN — virtual (shared bandwidth with SLA).

    The MEF (Metro Ethernet Forum) standardized these services — the framework that lets an operator offer "Ethernet as a service" with the SLA guarantees of traditional leased lines.

    4.3 Ring vs. mesh topologies¶

    Ring topology — nodes connected in a closed loop; traffic protection via a backup path (half the capacity reserved for protection).

    • Advantages: simple, sub-50 ms protection, predictable.
    • Disadvantages: half the capacity lost to protection.
    • Used in: legacy SDH/SONET, some modern metro Ethernet.

    Mesh topology — nodes connected in a partial mesh (multiple paths).

    • Advantages: full capacity available, better failure recovery with multiple paths, more flexible.
    • Disadvantages: more complex routing, more fiber.
    • Used in: modern IP/MPLS metro, leaf-spine data centers.

    The trend: ring → mesh as SDN makes mesh routing tractable.

    4.4 Aggregation for mobile¶

    Mobile aggregation (from gNBs to core) uses the same wired infrastructure as fixed broadband:

    • Fiber — preferred; carries high capacity with low latency.
    • Metro Ethernet — connects gNBs to the aggregation switch.
    • IP/MPLS — routes traffic to the mobile core (EPC or 5GC).

    Latency requirements (for 5G):

    • eMBB: < 10 ms to core (satisfied by metro fiber).
    • URLLC: < 1 ms to core (requires edge computing — MEC — see Chapter 6).
    • Sync: ±1.5 μs for TDD (via GPS, SyncE, or IEEE 1588 PTP).

    5. Mobile backhaul — the critical link¶

    5.1 The backhaul challenge¶

    Mobile backhaul connects cell sites to the core network. It is often the bottleneck in mobile networks — radio capacity grows faster than backhaul.

    The capacity gap:

    Generation Typical backhaul requirement per site
    2G (GSM) 2–10 Mbps (T1/E1 lines)
    3G (UMTS) 50–100 Mbps
    4G (LTE) 500 Mbps–2 Gbps
    4G (LTE-A, 3-sector, CA) 2–6 Gbps
    5G (sub-6, mid-band) 5–10 Gbps
    5G (mmWave, dense) 10–25 Gbps

    Each generation asks for 5–10× more backhaul — often ahead of what operators can deploy.

    5.2 Backhaul technologies¶

    Technology Capacity Distance Latency Cost per Mbps
    Fiber 1–100+ Gbps 40–80 km ~5 μs/km Lowest
    Microwave (6–42 GHz) 100 Mbps–3 Gbps 1–30 km (LOS) ~3 μs/km + processing Moderate
    E-band (70/80 GHz) 1–10 Gbps 1–3 km (LOS) low Moderate
    Satellite (GEO) 10–50 Mbps global 250–600 ms High
    Satellite (LEO) 50–200 Mbps global 30–60 ms Moderate–High
    DSL (copper) 5–100 Mbps 1–5 km Low Moderate

    Fiber is preferred when available: highest capacity, lowest latency, lowest cost-per-bit. But only ~20–30% of cell sites worldwide have fiber — the rest use microwave or other wireless backhaul.

    Microwave dominates rural backhaul — deployed in 30–60 days vs. months for fiber trenching, cheaper for low-to-moderate capacity. Modern microwave (E-band, 70–80 GHz) carries 1–10 Gbps — enough for 4G and much of 5G.

    Satellite is the last resort for remote sites (mountains, deserts, offshore). LEO (Starlink, OneWeb) is changing this — sub-60 ms latency, 100+ Mbps, competitive with rural microwave.

    Worked example 5.1 — backhaul sizing for a 5G site¶

    Scenario: A 5G gNB, 3 sectors, 100 MHz per sector (sub-6), 64T64R massive MIMO, peak 10 Gbps per sector.

    Peak per site: 3 × 10 = 30 Gbps.

    Busy-hour average: 40% of peak = 12 Gbps.

    Fiber backhaul: Deploy 100G or 4×10G fiber — overkill at current traffic but ready for growth.

    Alternative — E-band microwave: 4 × E-band links at 10 Gbps each = 40 Gbps aggregate. Higher cost, weather-dependent, requires LOS.

    Alternative — DSL for a small cell: Small cells (5G mmWave, 100–500 Mbps peak) can be backhauled by G.fast DSL (500 Mbps) — viable for dense urban deployment where fiber trenching is expensive.

    Rule of thumb: provision 1.5× the busy-hour average to allow for burst traffic and future growth. For our 5G site: ~20 Gbps backhaul as the initial deployment target.

    5.3 C-RAN backhaul — CPRI and eCPRI¶

    C-RAN (Chapter 5, §5.9.3) splits the base station into:

    • RRH (Remote Radio Head) — at the tower, RF + antennas.
    • BBU (Baseband Unit) — centralized in a BBU pool.

    The fronthaul between BBU and RRH carries digitized I/Q samples — much higher bandwidth than the user data.

    CPRI (Common Public Radio Interface) — the original fronthaul standard.

    CPRI bandwidth:

    \[ \boxed{\;R_{\text{CPRI}} = f_s \cdot N_{\text{bits}} \cdot 2 \cdot N_{\text{antennas}} \cdot \text{overhead}\;} \]

    For LTE 20 MHz: - I/Q sample rate: 30.72 Msps (2× 15.36 Msps for oversampling). - Bits per sample: 15 (typical). - I/Q components: 2. - CPRI line rate per antenna stream: 30.72 × 15 × 2 = 921.6 Mbps (plus overhead — CPRI options include 1.22, 2.43, 4.92, 9.83, 10.1, 24.3 Gbps).

    For a 3-sector, 4-antenna (MIMO) LTE site: 3 sectors × 2 antennas × 921.6 Mbps ≈ 5.5 Gbps per site (using CPRI option 7 at 9.83 Gbps line rate — with overhead and padding).

    For 5G NR 100 MHz, 64T64R massive MIMO: - Sample rate: 122.88 Msps. - Total I/Q streams: 64 antennas (each with its own I/Q). - CPRI bandwidth: ~50–100 Gbps per sector — way beyond what CPRI was designed for.

    eCPRI (enhanced CPRI) is the 5G evolution: it splits the PHY between RRH and BBU more flexibly — some PHY processing stays at the RRH, reducing fronthaul to ~10–25 Gbps for 100 MHz massive MIMO.

    Even eCPRI is high — this is why full C-RAN is deployed only in dense urban (with short fiber runs to RRHs) and not in rural areas (where fronthaul would be prohibitive).

    Worked example 5.2 — CPRI vs. user data¶

    Scenario: An LTE eNodeB with 3 sectors, 2×2 MIMO, 20 MHz, peak user data 150 Mbps.

    User data: 150 Mbps. CPRI bandwidth: ~5.5 Gbps.

    Ratio: CPRI carries 37× more bits than the actual user data — because it carries raw I/Q samples, not compressed data. This is the "fronthaul tax" of C-RAN.

    eCPRI partially corrects this by splitting at a different point: - Option split II (within PHY, after FFT) — ~2–4× reduction. - Option split II-D (after resource element mapping) — up to 10× reduction. - Option split I-D (higher-PHY function in RRH) — up to 20× reduction.

    Trade-off: more processing at the RRH (which has less compute and less cooling than a BBU pool) vs. lower fronthaul bandwidth.

    5.4 Timing and synchronization¶

    Mobile networks require precise timing — especially TDD systems where uplink and downlink share the same frequency and must align at cell edges.

    Requirements:

    • FDD LTE: ±50 ppb (parts per billion) — frequency only.
    • TDD LTE: ±1.5 μs absolute time — much stricter.
    • TDD 5G NR: ±1.5 μs absolute time, and ±130 ns for some features (e.g., carrier aggregation, CoMP).

    Where timing comes from:

    Source Accuracy Cost
    GPS/GNSS receiver < 100 ns Low (but antenna installation, vulnerability)
    IEEE 1588 PTP (Precision Time Protocol) < 1 μs (over fiber) Moderate
    SyncE (Synchronous Ethernet) < 10 ppb frequency Low (uses existing Ethernet)

    GNSS is the primary source at most cell sites — a GPS antenna on the tower provides time-of-day and frequency reference. But GNSS is vulnerable to jamming and spoofing — this is why many networks also use 1588 PTP over fiber as a backup.

    The trend: with 5G's tighter timing requirements and increasing GNSS vulnerability, operators are deploying IEEE 1588 PTP + SyncE as the primary timing for new sites — GNSS becomes the backup.

    6. The wired-wireless economics¶

    6.1 Cost per bit — the thousandfold difference¶

    Access technology Cost per GB
    Fiber (FTTH) ~$0.001–0.01
    Cable (DOCSIS 3.1) ~$0.01–0.05
    xDSL (VDSL2) ~$0.02–0.10
    Fixed wireless (5G FWA) ~$0.10–0.50
    Mobile 4G/5G ~$1–10

    The economic implication: operators want to offload as much traffic as possible to wireless LAN (WiFi) and fiber, keeping the expensive mobile radio for mobility-only traffic.

    This drives:

    • WiFi offload — 60–80% of smartphone data in urban areas is on WiFi, not cellular.
    • Fixed-mobile convergence — single core serving both fixed and mobile traffic.
    • Femtocells and small cells — cell at home = high-quality wireless at low cost.

    6.2 The "capacity vs. coverage" trade-off¶

    Wired and wireless have opposite economics:

    • Wired: high capex per home ($500–2000) but unlimited capacity and long life. Excellent economics at high density.
    • Wireless: lower capex per home ($50–500) but limited capacity and short spectrum/life. Excellent economics at low density.

    The crossover is around ~10–20 homes/km²:

    • Above — fiber is cheaper per home.
    • Below — wireless (FWA, satellite) is cheaper per home.

    This is why rural broadband is a worldwide problem — many rural areas are below the crossover density, so private fiber deployment doesn't pay back.

    Worked example 6.1 — fiber vs. FWA deployment¶

    Scenario: A village with 200 homes spread over 20 km².

    Fiber deployment: - Trench: ~15 km of new fiber routes. - Cost per km: \(30,000–\)80,000 (rural trenching is expensive). - Total trench cost: 15 × \(50,000 = **\)750,000. - Equipment per home (ONT): $200 × 200 = $40,000. - Total: \(790,000 → **\)3,950 per home.

    FWA deployment: - 2 base stations (for coverage): $80,000 each = $160,000. - CPE per home: $300 × 200 = $60,000. - Backhaul (fiber to each base station): $100,000. - Total: \(320,000 → **\)1,600 per home**.

    FWA wins at this density. Above ~1000 homes in the same 20 km², fiber economies kick in — the trench cost is amortized over more homes.

    6.3 Where the industry is heading¶

    Fiber everywhere it's economic:

    • FTTH in dense urban and new builds.
    • FTTC + G.fast in older areas (upgrade path to FTTH).
    • FWA for rural, where trenching is prohibitive.

    Convergence of fixed and mobile:

    • 5G core (5GC) supports fixed wireless access (FWA) via the same infrastructure as mobile — operators can serve homes with 5G.
    • WiFi 6/7 provides gigabit-class speeds in homes, backhauled by fiber.
    • Single IP network for all services.

    Software-defined everything:

    • SDN for core/transport.
    • NFV for network functions.
    • Cloud-native 5G core.
    • O-RAN for disaggregated radio.

    The wired network becomes invisible infrastructure — a cloud-like platform delivering connectivity for any wireless service.

    7. Chapter summary¶

    1. Every wireless call is mostly wired — the radio link is the last 100 m–1 km; everything else is fiber. The wired infrastructure sets the capacity, latency, and cost envelope for wireless.
    2. Access loop — the last-mile connection. xDSL (ADSL, VDSL2, G.fast) reuses copper; DOCSIS uses coax; PON (GPON 2.5 Gbps, XGS-PON 10 Gbps) is fiber to the home. FWA (5G at 3.5/28 GHz) is the wireless alternative for rural deployment.
    3. PON — passive splitters (1:32, 1:128) share bandwidth. GPON gives ~80 Mbps per user worst-case; XGS-PON gives ~300 Mbps; both have headroom for typical consumer needs.
    4. Core network — DWDM optical backbone, 100–400 Gbps per wavelength, 80+ wavelengths per fiber → 8–32 Tbps per fiber pair. OTN provides framing, FEC, and monitoring. IP/MPLS and Segment Routing route packets.
    5. SDN and NFV — the trend from hardware-centric to software-defined networks. Centralized controllers, virtualized network functions, orchestrated by MANO. Foundation of 5G core and O-RAN.
    6. Distribution (metro) networks — Carrier Ethernet (E-Line, E-LAN, E-Tree), ring or mesh topologies, aggregating access traffic into the core.
    7. Mobile backhaul — fiber is preferred (highest capacity, lowest latency, lowest cost-per-bit). ~70% of global cell sites use wireless backhaul (mostly microwave) because fiber isn't available. 5G sites need 5–25 Gbps — challenging for many sites.
    8. CPRI/eCPRI — C-RAN fronthaul carries I/Q samples at ~5–100 Gbps per site (37× the user data). eCPRI reduces this via different PHY splits.
    9. Timing — 5G TDD needs ±1.5 μs absolute time. GNSS is primary; IEEE 1588 PTP over fiber is backup. SyncE provides frequency synchronization.
    10. Cost per bit — wired is 100–1000× cheaper than wireless. This drives WiFi offload, FMC, and the push toward fiber + FWA deployment strategies.

    C. Define / explain — model answers
    1. Mostly wired: A 5G UE transmits to a gNB over a 100 m–1 km radio link. The gNB's backhaul is fiber (typically 5–100 km to the aggregation point). From aggregation to the mobile core is fiber (10–500 km). From the core to the internet is fiber (hundreds to thousands of km). Ratio of wired to wireless distance: 100–10,000×. The radio link is the "last hop" — the rest is fiber.
    2. xDSL: 100 Mbps (VDSL2 vectoring) to 1 Gbps (G.fast); 100 m–3 km; dedicated per line; low cost (reuses copper); good for existing copper. DOCSIS: 1 Gbps (3.0) to 10 Gbps (3.1); coax 500 m–2 km; shared among 500–2000 homes per node; low cost where coax exists; good for cable operators. PON: 2.5 Gbps (GPON) to 10 Gbps (XGS-PON) or 40 Gbps (NG-PON2); 20 km reach; shared among 32–128 subscribers per splitter; moderate cost (fiber); best for new builds and dense areas. Best for: rural → FWA or xDSL; urban → PON; new build → PON.
    3. PON architecture: OLT at the exchange → passive splitter → ONU at the subscriber. The splitter is a passive optical device — no power, no electronics, no maintenance. This is critical because it puts no active equipment in the field — no cabinet, no batteries, no cooling. Only the OLT (at the exchange) and the ONU (at the home) need power. This is what makes FTTH affordable.
    4. DWDM: WDM multiplexes multiple wavelengths on one fiber. DWDM uses 50/100 GHz spacing → 40–80+ channels. Each channel carries 100–400 Gbps with coherent optics. Total: 80 × 200 Gbps = 16 Tbps per fiber pair. That's enough for the entire traffic of a mid-sized country.
    5. Layered core network: (a) Fiber — physical medium, single-mode for long-haul. (b) DWDM — multiplexes wavelengths, provides huge capacity. (c) OTN — wraps client signals with FEC, monitoring, protection, multiplexing. (d) IP/MPLS — packet routing, VPN, traffic engineering, QoS. Each layer provides a distinct function and abstraction.
    6. SDN: decouples control plane (centralized controller) from data plane (forwarding hardware). Enables centralized optimization, programmability, fast deployment. NFV: virtualizes network functions (routers, firewalls, EPC) onto commodity servers. Enables flexible scaling, fast deployment, lower capex. 5G use: 5G core is cloud-native, service-based, with NFV and SDN providing the deployment platform. Network functions (AMF, SMF, UPF) are virtualized, run in cloud/edge data centers, orchestrated by Kubernetes. This is what enables network slicing, MEC, and rapid service deployment.
    7. Ring: nodes connected in a loop, protection via a backup path (half capacity reserved). Simple, sub-50 ms protection, but wastes half the capacity on protection. Mesh: multiple paths between nodes; full capacity available; more flexible. Used in modern IP/MPLS metro with SDN — the controller computes optimal paths and handles failure recovery. Trend: ring → mesh as SDN makes mesh routing tractable.
    8. Backhaul options: fiber (highest capacity, lowest latency), microwave (1–3 Gbps, needs LOS, weather-dependent), E-band (1–10 Gbps, short range), satellite (10–200 Mbps, high latency for GEO, moderate for LEO), DSL (small cells). Why mostly wireless: fiber is expensive to deploy (trenching costs $30K–80K per km in rural areas); global fiber coverage is only 20–30% of cell sites; microwave deploys in 30–60 days; many rural sites are below the economic threshold for fiber.
    9. C-RAN: centralizes BBUs in a pool; RRHs at the tower; fronthaul between them. CPRI: carries digitized I/Q samples, not user data. For LTE 20 MHz, CPRI carries ~5.5 Gbps per site — 37× the user data (150 Mbps peak). Why the tax: the RRH doesn't process the signal — it just converts between RF and digital. All processing happens at the BBU pool. The digitized RF samples have huge dynamic range and oversampling — 15-bit I/Q at 30.72 Msps per antenna × many antennas. This gives C-RAN flexibility (all baseband processing centralized) but requires expensive fiber fronthaul. eCPRI reduces this by splitting PHY functions between RRH and BBU.
    10. IEEE 1588 PTP: distributes precise time (not just frequency) over Ethernet. Master/slave protocol with hardware timestamping in network switches. Accuracy < 1 μs over fiber; better with careful engineering. Why critical: TDD systems use the same frequency for uplink and downlink; if cells are not time-synchronized, uplink transmissions from one cell interfere with downlink transmissions in neighboring cells. FDD doesn't have this problem. TDD also needs timing for carrier aggregation, CoMP, and other features. ±1.5 μs is the requirement — GNSS provides this at most sites; PTP is the backup.
    11. Cost per bit: wired ~\(0.001–0.01/GB, wireless ~\)1–10/GB. 100–1000× difference. Impact: (i) operators push traffic to WiFi (60–80% of smartphone data in urban areas is on WiFi); (ii) fixed-mobile convergence — unified core for both; (iii) femtocells — cellular at home, cheaper than macro; (iv) 5G FWA vs. FTTH — choose based on density; (v) pricing models — mobile data is priced to discourage heavy use, fiber is unlimited.
    12. 500 homes, 30 km²: density ~17 homes/km². Fiber cost: 20 km of trench at $50K/km = $1M; ONTs $200 × 500 = \(100K; total ~\)1.1M → $2,200/home. 5G FWA cost: 2 base stations at $100K = $200K; CPE $300 × 500 = $150K; backhaul \(150K; total ~\)500K → **\(1,000/home**. *FWA wins* at this density. *Break-even:* at ~1000 homes in the same 30 km² (~33 homes/km²), fiber cost per home drops to ~\)1200 (trench amortized) — approaching FWA. Above ~1500 homes, fiber is clearly cheaper and higher-capacity. Below ~20 homes/km², both are uneconomical — satellite or government subsidy needed.

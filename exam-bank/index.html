    Exam Bank — ECEG-6412 Recent Trends in Communication Technologies¶

    40 questions spanning all seven chapters plus the wired-technology chapter. Use for self-assessment and exam preparation. Answers and grading rubric at the end.

    Instructions (for a mock exam):

    • Time: 3 hours
    • Closed book; one A4 handwritten formula sheet allowed
    • Non-programmable calculator permitted
    • Total marks: 100
    • Marks per question in brackets

    Section A — Introduction (Ch 1) — 15 marks¶

    A1. Multiple choice [2]¶

    In a hexagonal cellular system with cluster size \(N = 7\) and path-loss exponent \(n = 4\), the approximate omni-cell SIR is:

    (a) 10 dB (b) 13 dB (c) 18.7 dB (d) 30 dB

    A2. Multiple choice [2]¶

    Log-normal shadowing in an urban environment has a typical standard deviation of:

    (a) 1 dB (b) 3 dB (c) 6–10 dB (d) 25 dB

    A3. Multiple choice [2]¶

    Which 3GPP protocol layer is responsible for HARQ retransmissions?

    (a) PHY (b) MAC (c) RRC (d) NAS

    A4. Short answer [3]¶

    State the free-space path-loss (Friis) formula in dB, and explain why mmWave (28 GHz) has ~30 dB more path loss at 1 km than sub-1 GHz.

    A5. Short answer [3]¶

    Define coherence bandwidth \(B_c\) and coherence time \(T_c\). Explain what type of fading the system experiences when the signal bandwidth \(B_s \gg B_c\).

    A6. Derivation [3]¶

    For a two-tier hexagonal cellular system, derive the approximate SIR expression \(\text{SIR} \approx (3N)^{n/2}/6\) (or state assumptions and result). What does it say about the trade-off between \(N\) and capacity?

    Section B — Modulation and Coding (Ch 2) — 20 marks¶

    B1. Multiple choice [2]¶

    At the same \(E_b/N_0\), QPSK gives:

    (a) Half the BER of BPSK (b) The same BER as BPSK (c) Twice the BER of BPSK (d) Unrelated BER

    B2. Multiple choice [2]¶

    The spectral efficiency of QPSK with an ideal Nyquist pulse (\(\beta = 0\)) is:

    (a) 1 bps/Hz (b) 2 bps/Hz (c) 4 bps/Hz (d) 6 bps/Hz

    B3. Multiple choice [2]¶

    OFDM handles frequency-selective fading by:

    (a) Using a Rake receiver (b) Using higher power (c) Splitting the channel into flat subcarriers (d) Spreading across the time axis

    B4. Short answer [3]¶

    State the Shannon capacity formula. For a 20 MHz channel at SNR = 15 dB, compute the maximum achievable rate.

    B5. Short answer [3]¶

    Explain Gray coding and why it reduces BER at the same symbol error rate.

    B6. Derivation [4]¶

    Compute the peak HSDPA downlink rate (R5) for: - 15 HS-PDSCH codes, SF = 16 - 16-QAM modulation - Rate-3/4 coding - Chip rate = 3.84 Mcps

    Show all steps.

    B7. Short answer [4]¶

    State the cycle prefix in OFDM. Why must \(T_{cp} > \tau_{\max}\)? What is the throughput cost of the CP (formula and typical percentage)?

    Section C — Wired Technology Trends — 5 marks¶

    C1. Multiple choice [2]¶

    GPON downstream capacity is:

    (a) 155 Mbps (b) 2.5 Gbps (c) 10 Gbps (d) 40 Gbps

    C2. Short answer [3]¶

    Explain why every wireless call is "mostly wired." Give one reason the wired backhaul is often the bottleneck in 5G networks.

    Section D — GSM (Ch 3) — 15 marks¶

    D1. Multiple choice [2]¶

    GSM uses which multiple-access technique?

    (a) FDMA (b) TDMA (c) CDMA (d) OFDMA

    D2. Multiple choice [2]¶

    GSM's bit rate on the air interface (per 200 kHz carrier) is:

    (a) 33.85 kbps (b) 100 kbps (c) 270.833 kbps (d) 1 Mbps

    D3. Short answer [3]¶

    Describe the three burst types in GSM (Normal, Frequency Correction, Access) and the purpose of each.

    D4. Short answer [3]¶

    Explain the RPE-LTP speech codec at 13 kbps. What are its three main components, and how do they contribute to the compression?

    D5. Derivation [5]¶

    For a GSM TDMA frame with 8 slots of 577 μs each: (a) Compute the total gross bit rate per carrier. (b) Compute the number of voice channels per cell if \(N = 4\) and there are 125 total carriers across 25 MHz uplink. (c) Compare with AMPS at \(N = 7\), 30 kHz channels. What is the capacity multiplier?

    Section E — UMTS (Ch 4) — 15 marks¶

    E1. Multiple choice [2]¶

    UMTS FDD uses which multiple-access technique?

    (a) TDMA (b) FDMA (c) WCDMA (d) OFDMA

    E2. Multiple choice [2]¶

    The chip rate of UMTS FDD is:

    (a) 1.2288 Mcps (b) 3.84 Mcps (c) 5 Mcps (d) 20 Mcps

    E3. Short answer [3]¶

    Explain the Rake receiver. Why is multipath a diversity in WCDMA, not a problem (as in GSM)?

    E4. Short answer [3]¶

    Describe soft handover in UMTS. What is the active set, and why is soft handover a signature advantage of CDMA?

    E5. Derivation [5]¶

    Compute the pole capacity for UMTS voice at 12.2 kbps (AMR), with: - Chip rate \(W = 3.84\) MHz - \((E_b/N_0)_{\text{req}} = 5\) dB - Inter-cell interference factor \(i = 0.5\) - Activity factor \(v = 0.6\)

    Then compute realistic capacity at 50% of pole.

    H1. Multiple choice [2]¶

    Federated learning avoids:

    (a) Training (b) Centralizing raw data (c) Model updates (d) Gradient computation

    H2. Short answer [3]¶

    Answer key¶

    Click to reveal answers

    Section A¶

    A1. (c) 18.7 dB — \((21)^2/6 \approx 73.5 \approx 18.7\) dB.

    A2. (c) 6–10 dB urban.

    A3. (b) MAC — HARQ is a MAC-layer function.

    A4. \(\text{FSPL}_{\text{dB}} = 32.44 + 20\log_{10}(f_{\text{MHz}}) + 20\log_{10}(d_{\text{km}})\). At 900 MHz, 1 km: 91.5 dB. At 28 GHz, 1 km: 121.4 dB. Difference: 30 dB. The \(20\log f\) term captures the fact that FSPL increases as \(f^2\) — higher frequencies spread the same energy over a larger aperture for a fixed antenna gain.

    A5. Coherence bandwidth: the frequency range over which the channel response is approximately flat (highly correlated). \(B_c \approx 1/(5\tau_{\text{rms}})\) (50% correlation). Coherence time: the time over which the channel is approximately constant. \(T_c \approx 0.423/f_D\). When \(B_s \gg B_c\), the channel is frequency-selective — needs equalization (or OFDM).

    A6. For 6 first-tier interferers at distance \(D\), and desired signal at distance \(R\) (edge of cell), SIR = \(R^{-n} / \sum_i D_i^{-n} \approx R^{-n}/(6 D^{-n})\) if all interferers are at approximately \(D\). Then SIR = \((D/R)^n/6 = (3N)^{n/2}/6\). Trade-off: smaller \(N\) increases capacity (more reuse) but decreases SIR (more interference). Design must choose \(N\) such that the SIR meets the modulation/coding target.

    Section B¶

    B1. (b) Same BER as BPSK.

    B2. (b) 2 bps/Hz.

    B3. (c) Split the channel into flat subcarriers.

    B4. \(C = B\log_2(1 + \text{SNR})\). For \(B = 20\) MHz, SNR = 15 dB (linear 31.62): \(C = 20\times10^6 \times \log_2(32.62) \approx 20\times10^6 \times 5.03 \approx 100.6\) Mbps.

    B5. Gray coding: a mapping from bits to constellation symbols such that adjacent constellation points differ by exactly 1 bit. When a symbol decision error lands on an adjacent point (the dominant error type at high SNR), the bit error count is 1, not \(\log_2 M\). This reduces the BER at the same SER by a factor of approximately \(\log_2 M\).

    B6. Per code: \(3.84\) Mcps / 16 = 240 ksym/s × 4 bits/sym (16-QAM) × \(0.75\) (rate-3/4) = 720 kbps. × 15 codes = 10.8 Mbps (practical) or 14.4 Mbps (rate-1 theoretical). Standard says 14.4 Mbps is the theoretical peak; realistic is ~10 Mbps.

    B7. CP: copies the last \(T_{cp}\) seconds of the useful symbol to the front. Why \(T_{cp} > \tau_{\max}\): to absorb the delay-spread tail of the previous symbol entirely within the CP → zero ISI after discarding the CP. Cost: throughput reduced by \(\eta_{CP} = T_u/(T_u + T_{cp})\), typically ~7% for LTE normal CP.

    Section C¶

    C1. (b) 2.5 Gbps.

    C2. Mostly wired: the radio link from UE to base station is only 100 m–1 km; the backhaul to the core is fiber over 10s to 1000s of km. Ratio is 100–10,000× more wired than wireless. Bottleneck: 5G sites need 5–25 Gbps backhaul (fiber), but only ~30% of global cell sites have fiber — most rely on microwave or slower links, so the radio can transmit more than the network can deliver.

    Section D¶

    D1. (b) TDMA.

    D2. (c) 270.833 kbps.

    D3. Normal burst: carries traffic and most control, 156.25 bits with training sequence for channel estimation. Frequency correction burst: all zeros, produces a pure tone for frequency tuning. Access burst: shorter (88 bits) with long guard, used on RACH for random access (unknown propagation delay).

    D4. RPE-LTP: (a) LPC analysis — 8 LPC coefficients from Yule-Walker on each 20 ms frame, quantized as LARs (36 bits). (b) LTP — long-term prediction removes pitch-period structure; lag (7 bits) + gain (2 bits) per subframe × 4 = 36 bits. (c) RPE — remaining residual downsampled to 13 samples per subframe with grid position (2 bits) and samples (39 bits) = 41 bits × 4 = 164 bits. Total 236 bits per 20 ms; padded to 260; 260 × 50 = 13 kbps.

    D5. (a) 8 × 156.25 bits per 4.615 ms = 1250 bits / 4.615 ms = 270.833 kbps. (b) 25 MHz / 0.2 MHz = 125 carriers. With \(N = 4\): 125/4 ≈ 31 carriers per cell. 8 voice channels per carrier → 248 voice channels per cell. (c) AMPS: 25 MHz / 30 kHz ≈ 833 carriers; with \(N = 7\): 833/7 ≈ 119 per cell. Ratio = 248/119 ≈ 2.08× — GSM has ~2× the voice capacity of AMPS.

    Section E¶

    E1. (c) WCDMA.

    E2. (b) 3.84 Mcps.

    E3. Rake receiver: multiple finger demodulators, each locked to one multipath delay; outputs are maximal-ratio combined. Why diversity: wideband CDMA's chip duration is much shorter than typical delay spreads, so multipath components appear as distinguishable copies rather than overlapping ISI. The Rake exploits this as diversity (3-path diversity ≈ 4.77 dB gain in Rayleigh fading), whereas in narrowband systems (like GSM) the same multipath causes ISI and requires an equalizer.

    E4. Soft handover: a UE is connected to 2 or more NodeBs simultaneously, combining signals. Active set: the set of cells currently connected. Why signature advantage: the UE keeps the old link while establishing the new — no break-before-make interruption. This is enabled by \(N=1\) universal frequency reuse; the UE can receive/transmit on the same frequency from multiple cells. GSM (with \(N>1\)) requires hard handover between frequencies.

    E5. Pole capacity: \(N_{\text{pole}} = 1 + W/(R \cdot (E_b/N_0)_{\text{req}} \cdot (1+i) \cdot v)\). - \(W = 3.84\) MHz. - \(R = 12.2\) kbps. - \((E_b/N_0)_{\text{req}} = 5\) dB = 3.16 linear. - \(i = 0.5\). - \(v = 0.6\) (activity factor). \(N_{\text{pole}} = 1 + 3.84\times10^6 / (12.2\times10^3 \cdot 3.16 \cdot 1.5 \cdot 0.6) \approx 1 + 110 \approx 111\). Realistic capacity at 50% of pole: ~55 voice users per cell.

    Section F¶

    F1. (b) SC-FDMA.

    F2. (b) 15 kHz.

    F3. DL OFDMA: the eNodeB is mains-powered (no battery constraint) and benefits from OFDMA's flexibility for frequency-selective scheduling. UL SC-FDMA: the UE is battery-powered; OFDMA's high PAPR (10–12 dB) would require 10+ dB PA backoff → inefficient → short battery + poor coverage. SC-FDMA's DFT-precoding reduces PAPR to 3–6 dB → UE PA operates near saturation → ~6 dB uplink power gain → ~2× cell radius.

    F4. EPC architecture: (i) eNodeB — base station, all radio functions (PHY, MAC, RLC, PDCP, RRC). (ii) MME — control-plane: NAS signalling, authentication, mobility, bearer setup. (iii) S-GW — user-plane anchor across handovers, charging. (iv) P-GW — gateway to external PDN, IP allocation, QoS enforcement. (v) HSS — subscription data + authentication vectors. Key interfaces: Uu (UE-eNodeB), S1-MME (eNodeB-MME), S1-U (eNodeB-S-GW), S5/S8 (S-GW-P-GW), S6a (MME-HSS), X2 (eNodeB-eNodeB).

    F5. - Per RB per subframe: 12 subcarriers × 7 symbols × 2 slots = 168 REs. With 20% RS overhead: ~134 usable REs. - Per RE: 64-QAM (6 bits) × 3/4 = 4.5 bits. Per RB per subframe: 134 × 4.5 = 603 bits. - Per subframe: 100 RBs × 603 = 60,300 bits. - Per second (1000 subframes): 60.3 Mbps per stream. - 2×2 MIMO: 2 streams = 120.6 Mbps. - Compare to standard Cat-4 150 Mbps: the standard assumes lower RS overhead and slightly more aggressive assumptions. 120 Mbps is a realistic practical peak.

    Section G¶

    G1. (c) 802.15.4.

    G2. (c) Chirp Spread Spectrum.

    G3. LoRa: 868/915 MHz unlicensed ISM; 0.3–37.5 kbps; 2–15 km; 10+ year battery. Best for: private enterprise IoT (agriculture, remote sensing). NB-IoT: Licensed LTE bands (700/800/900 MHz); 26–127 kbps; 1–10 km; 5–10 year battery (PSM). Best for: operator-managed smart metering. Sigfox: 868/902 MHz ISM; ~100 bps; 3–50 km; 10+ year battery. Best for: ultra-low-rate asset tracking (logistics, simple telemetry).

    Section H¶

    H1. (b) Centralizing raw data.

    H2. Autoencoder: encoder (transmitter) maps bits to channel symbols, a differentiable channel model, decoder (receiver) maps back to bits. Trained end-to-end to minimize BER/bit-error loss. Critical requirement: the channel must be differentiable for backpropagation. What it discovers: on AWGN, learns QPSK (matches classical optimum); on non-linear channel (e.g., PA distortion), learns a constellation that compensates for the non-linearity — beating classical QAM which was optimized for AWGN.

    H3. Example challenge: the model-reality gap — training on 3GPP TR 38.901 synthetic channels vs. deploying on real measured channels. Why harder than vision/NLP: the "data distribution" for communications is highly site-specific and non-stationary — a model trained on one channel (e.g., urban macro) may fail completely on another (e.g., indoor, high-speed train). Vision/NLP have relatively stable data distributions (natural images, language) that generalize much better.

    Grading rubric (for instructors)¶

    Band Marks Criteria
    A 90–100 All questions correct or with trivial arithmetic slips; derivations rigorous; interpretation insightful
    B 75–89 Most questions correct; occasional conceptual gaps; derivations mostly sound
    C 60–74 Basic understanding demonstrated; some errors in derivations or interpretation
    F < 60 Major conceptual gaps; significant derivations missing

    Point allocations: MCQs all-or-nothing; short answers partial credit per correct step; derivations partial credit per correct line.

    Common error checks (and deductions):

    • Wrong sign in a formula (e.g., \(-20\log\) instead of \(+20\log\)): −1 to −2.
    • **Confusing bps/Hz with raw bit rate: −2.
    • **Missing \(N\) or \(i\) in pole-capacity formula: −2.
    • Forgetting CP overhead in OFDM calculation: −1 to −2.
    • **Confusing SC-FDMA and OFDMA in uplink/downlink: −2.
    • Claiming soft handover is impossible in GSM (hard handover only): −2.
    • **Missing MIMO doubling factor in LTE peak calculation: −2.
    • **Confusing TDMA and CDMA when stating GSM/UMTS multiple access: −3.
